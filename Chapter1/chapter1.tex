\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Overview}

The growing demand for energy, and the requirement that it be supplied without
carbon emissions, is leading governments and companies to look for solutions
beyond the ones in service today. In this context the nuclear industry has put
forward a number of innovative designs, with the aim of improving the safety and
the efficiency of the plant and of extending its field of application beyond the
production of electricity, to district heating, to the production of hydrogen
and to the desalination of water.

Nearly all of these designs share one strategy: the size of the core, and with
it the power of the unit, is reduced. This means that the new designs give up
the economy of scale on which large plants have relied. On the other hand, a
small unit allows most of its components, and in the smallest
designs the unit itself, to be built in a factory and installed on site already
assembled, so that the same object is produced many times over and the
experience gained on one unit is carried to the next. Construction on site is
correspondingly shorter, which matters twice over, since the capital borrowed to
build the plant is tied up for less time before the plant begins to earn.
Whether these advantages compensate the loss of scale is a question of its own,
and one on which the size of the reactor is only one of the terms that
count~\cite{smrEconomics}. What the reduced size does open is a market that a
large plant has never served: sites off the grid, remote communities, mining
operations, ports and data centres, where the term of
comparison is a diesel generator~\cite{hpmrMarkets,econByDesign}.

Westinghouse Electric Company has proposed two innovative designs along these
lines. The first is a lead-cooled fast reactor of \SI{950}{MW_{th}}, developed
with Argonne National Laboratory~\cite{wecLFR}, which belongs to the fourth
generation of power reactors and aims at a better use of the fuel and at a
reduced burden of long-lived waste. The second is the eVinci micro reactor, of a
few megawatts and transportable, in which the heat is removed by alkali-metal
heat pipes rather than by a circulating coolant~\cite{eVinci}.

Designs of this kind have little or no operating experience behind them, and their
performance has to be accurately predicted, both while the design is being settled and
when it is presented for licensing, by calculation. Experiments remain
indispensable, and are neither quick nor cheap, so that the computing power now
available is used to reduce their number and to support the design of
experiments. The tools that answer these questions, however, were for the most part
built for light-water reactors, and they do not transfer unchanged to fast-spectrum
designs or to a core made of heat pipes and hydride moderators. This thesis is
thus concerned more with the computational methods than with a single design: it develops
techniques that improve the simulation of concepts of these two kinds, working
on models that are in the public domain and stating in each case how far the
result carries beyond the core on which it was obtained.

The two families to which these designs belong are the subject of this thesis,
one in each of its Parts. Lead-cooled fast reactors (LFRs) operate at low
pressure and at temperatures higher than those of light-water reactors, and
their fast spectrum fissions plutonium and the minor actinides, so that the same
core produces energy and reduces the long-lived waste. Part~\ref{part:lfr}
considers two designs, a simplified model of the Westinghouse
LFR~\cite{wecLFR} and the ALFRED core, the Advanced Lead Fast Reactor
European Demonstrator, in the specification of the benchmark coordinated by the Nuclear
Energy Agency~\cite{neaBench}. Heat-pipe micro
reactors (HPMRs), the subject of Part~\ref{part:hpmr}, are cores of a few
megawatts in which heat pipes take the place of the circulating coolant and the
reactivity is governed by drums rotating in the radial reflector. Since the
industrial designs of this class are not public in the detail that a
neutronic study requires, the reactor considered is the micro reactor developed
at Argonne National Laboratory as a modelling exercise meant to collect in one
core the features of this reactor, i.e., heat pipes, tristructural-isotropic
(TRISO) fuel,
yttrium-hydride moderator pins and control drums~\cite{Stauff2024}, whose models
are distributed on the Virtual Test Bed platform~\cite{moose_vtb_mrad_description}.

Both designs share a difficulty of the same nature. The computational chain that decades
of light-water reactor practice have consolidated proceeds from the point-wise
nuclear data to a few-group set of homogenized constants~\cite{cacuci2010handbook}
and from there to a coarse-mesh nodal solution of the whole
core~\cite{Lawrence1986,Smith1986}. On it rest the core simulators with which
those plants are designed and the online monitoring systems with which they are
operated~\cite{BoydMiller1996}, and its accuracy rests on an equivalence between
the heterogeneous assembly and its homogenized representation that was
established for light-water lattices~\cite{Smith1986}. Neither family inherits
that chain as it stands.

In a fast system the choices that light-water practice
settled long ago, the environment in which the weighting flux is computed and
the grid over which the energy is condensed, no longer follow from that practice
and have to be examined again. The computing power now available makes it
possible to examine and compute them on the design itself, with the group
constants generated by a Monte Carlo calculation of the core rather than by the
chain of lattice steps, which is the route taken in Part~\ref{part:lfr}. This
does not supersede the lattice chain, which remains the established route of
industrial practice and is not easily replaced at that scale; the Monte Carlo
generation is affordable here because the constants are produced once, for a
design under study.

For a
micro reactor, those who develop the reactor physics tools within the Nuclear
Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program observe that heat
pipes, hydride moderators, TRISO fuel and control drums call
for a geometrical generality that the traditional nodal methods do not
possess~\cite{Wang2025griffin,Stauff2024}, that the homogenized solution departs
from the heterogeneous one more than it does in light-water
reactors~\cite{Pandya2022}, and that the cross sections have to be tabulated
against the angle of the drums~\cite{Roskoff2022physor}. With no operating
experience to appeal to, the reference against which a model of either family is
judged is the explicit Monte Carlo solution of the full core.

\section{Aim of the work and outline of the thesis}

The neutronic characterization of a reactor is not obtained from a single state
of the core, but from a large number of them. Exploring a design space means
computing the core at the positions the control system can assume, at different
temperatures and at successive depletion levels of the fuel; following a fuel cycle means
computing a sequence of states; monitoring an operating core means predicting
its critical configuration continuously as the core evolves. A Monte Carlo simulation of the critical state
of the micro reactor of Part~\ref{part:hpmr} costs several tens of CPU hours, an
expense that is justified if the study requires only a few states, while a study
of thousands does not.
Every practical scheme therefore places, between the reference calculation and
the many evaluations, a set of precomputed input data: prepared once from a few
reference calculations, and used many times in their place. In
Part~\ref{part:lfr} these data are the multi-group constants feeding a
deterministic solver. In Part~\ref{part:hpmr} they are a database of
fission matrices, tallied at a set of reference states, from which any
intermediate state is recovered by interpolation and by the solution of an
eigenvalue problem of modest size~\cite{CarneyANE2014,WaltersNSE2018}.

Preparing such data means averaging, and averaging means disregarding some
details. The
multi-group constants retain a group constant collapsed over each energy group
and homogenized over each region of the core; the
database retains the fission matrix only at the states at which it was tallied. Whatever physical detail is discarded at
this stage is not available to any calculation that uses the data afterwards,
and a more accurate solver does not bring it back. The quality of these data
therefore sets a limit on everything computed from them, which is the reason
this thesis focuses more on the data than on the solvers.

This thesis aims at improving these data in two ways. The first is the way they are
prepared. In Part~\ref{part:lfr} this means the route by which the group
constants are generated and the energy grid on which they are condensed; in
Part~\ref{part:hpmr}, the parameters the database spans, how densely it covers
them and how its fission matrices are interpolated between one entry and the next. The
second way is to correct the data once they are prepared. The correction is
computed from a small number of reference calculations, and is applied so that
the corrected data reproduce, at the conditions of the reference, what the
reference itself gives: the superhomogenization factors~\cite{Hbert_1980} in the
first Part, and in the second the correction ratios for the FM, taken element by element
between two matrices tallied at conditions that differ in a single effect. Both act on the
data and not on the solver, which receives corrected input and requires no
modification, and it is in this sense that the approaches proposed here are
non-intrusive. Throughout, the reference keeps the last word: no result rests on
the precomputed data alone, and where a faster approximation of the solver is
used to explore the alternatives, it is the reference solver that is used to judge what the
exploration returns. The reference is itself a
statistical estimate and is treated as one, so that two structures whose
predictions differ by less than its uncertainty are not held to be one better
than the other, and the noise that the tallied fission matrices carry is
propagated to every result obtained from them.

The aim of this thesis is to improve the accuracy, containing the cost, of
the neutronic analysis of two advanced reactor concepts by acting on the data
that stand between the Monte Carlo (MC) reference and the
calculations that their design and operation require. For the lead-cooled fast
reactor these data are the multi-group constants of a deterministic solver,
and the work concerns the route by which they are generated, their
correction and the energy grid on which they are condensed. For the heat-pipe
micro reactor it is a database of fission matrices, and the work concerns the
construction of the database, its use for the search of the critical state and
the corrections that extend it to the conditions of an operating core. The
thesis is organized in two Parts, each opened by a chapter that provides the
theory common to the works it contains, and closed by a chapter of conclusions
common to both.

Chapter~\ref{ch:part1-opener} opens Part~\ref{part:lfr} and lays the common
ground of its two works: the passage from the transport equation to its
multi-group form, the definition of the group constants as flux-weighted
averages over an energy interval and over a region of the core, the assumptions
that this definition requires and the errors it introduces. Two of the degrees
of freedom that the definition leaves open, the environment in which the
weighting flux is computed and the grid over which the energy is condensed, are
the subjects of the two chapters that follow. In both, the multi-group
constants and the reference solution come from the same Serpent~2 calculations.

Chapter~\ref{ch:sph-lfr} addresses the Westinghouse LFR design. Its constants
are MC-based and generated along two routes, either from a single full-core
model or from a set of mini-core models each representing one region of the
system, and they feed a
diffusion solver written in FreeFEM++ on a finite-element mesh.
Superhomogenization factors are then computed for the regions responsible for
the largest discrepancies in the power distribution, and the two routes, with
and without correction, are compared on the multiplication factor, the power
distribution and the kinetic parameters, and on the computational time each
requires when several configurations of the control rods have to be analysed.

Chapter~\ref{ch:energy-grid} addresses the ALFRED core design and the energy grid
itself. The boundaries of the few-group structure are the unknowns of an
optimization: a genetic algorithm searches the subsets of the boundaries of a
thirty-group fine grid for the structure whose deterministic solution aims at
reproducing, in three configurations of the control rods at once, the
multiplication factor, the kinetic parameters, the power distribution and the
flux distribution of the reference. The work that
formulated the problem had run three searches from three random seeds and
obtained three different structures; this chapter runs one thousand. That
number is within reach because a neural network trained on fewer than one
hundred thousand full-core evaluations of the deterministic code FRENETIC
stands in for the code inside the search, while every structure the searches
return is recomputed with the code. The chapter measures how strongly the
outcome depends on the seed and how many searches are needed, identifies the
boundaries that the optimized structures have in common, compares the best of
them with the structures obtained with the solver inside the loop and with
structures built by standard spacing rules, and states the limits within which
the result holds.

Chapter~\ref{ch:fm-theory} opens Part~\ref{part:hpmr}. It derives the fission
matrix from the transport equation, reviews the strategies by which the matrix
can be generated, describes the reactor model and sets up the framework that the
two following chapters share: the parametrization of the database over the
operating state, the treatment of the statistical uncertainty of the
interpolated results and the metrics by which the errors are measured.

Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} applies that framework to the criticality search, that
is, to find the angle of the control drums that makes the core critical at a
given power and at a given stage of the fuel cycle, together with the fission
source and the temperature field of that state. A database of fission
matrices is built over two parameters, i.e., the fuel temperature and the drum
angle, its density is fixed by a convergence study, and a correlation between
power and fuel temperature stands in for the thermal solver, so that the search runs on the
fission matrix alone. The algorithm is verified against reference Monte Carlo
calculations at beginning of life and then carried along the fuel cycle by
means of the Burnup Correction Ratio; the assumptions on which that extension
rests are examined one by one, together with the density of the snapshot grid
and the computational cost of the whole procedure.

Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios} removes the restriction to isothermal
states. The database of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} considers every material at a
single temperature, and two phenomena typical of an operating core lie outside
its reach, a
reflector colder than the core and a temperature that varies sharply from one
cell to the next. Each receives a correction ratio built in analogy to the
burnup correction, with a weight that scales the ratio by the fraction of the
reference effect present at the target state: the Reflector Correction Ratio,
developed on the three-dimensional model of the criticality search, and the
Core Temperature Correction Ratio, developed on a two-dimensional variant of
the same design. The chapter also carries out the analysis of the statistical
uncertainty of the interpolated matrix:
the correlation among the tallied coefficients is modelled, its invariance with
temperature is examined and the resulting estimate is verified against
independent replicas of the database, before the results of the core
temperature correction are read against the combined uncertainty.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} gathers the conclusions of the two Parts and the
future works and developments that the work leaves open.
