\chapter{Group Constants Generation and SPH Correction for the Westinghouse LFR}\label{ch:sph-lfr}

\section{Introduction}

Interest in Lead-cooled Fast Reactor (LFR) technology has been growing. Liquid lead lets the plant run at low pressure and at higher temperatures than Light Water Reactors (LWRs), and the fast spectrum fissions plutonium and the minor actinides more efficiently, easing the long-term waste burden. Several companies, Westinghouse among them, are developing their own LFR designs.

A large part of the neutronics work on these designs passes through the generation of multi-group constants. With the computational power available today, Monte Carlo (MC) codes have become a natural tool for collapsing and homogenizing nuclear data: they treat energy and angle as continuous variables and model the geometry of the system in full detail. Two strategies are commonly used to produce multi-group data with a MC code. The first relies on a set of assembly-level models, each meant to represent a portion of the core in its own conditions; the second uses a single full-core model, divided into the regions over which the continuous data are collapsed and homogenized. This chapter uses both and compares them.

Multi-group data typically feed deterministic solvers, which run in a fraction of the time of a MC calculation but rest on assumptions and simplifications that can degrade the accuracy of the results. The deterministic model developed for this chapter is a diffusion solver: it works on multi-group data homogenized at the assembly level, discretizes space with the Finite Element Method (FEM), and solves the neutron diffusion equation. To improve its output, SPH correction factors \cite{Hbert_1980} are applied to the group constants of the material regions responsible for the largest discrepancy in the power distribution of the LFR design. The SPH method has already been applied to liquid metal fast reactor (LMFR) designs \cite{luo2024fullcore_sph,Nikitin_mini_core_models,Rachamin2018}; those studies serve as the reference for the present one, whose objective is to bring the technique to the Westinghouse LFR design and to analyze the trends obtained with the different MGXS generation strategies, with and without the correction. A second objective is to show how little the SPH approach demands of the host code: no structural modification, only a straightforward two-step workflow, which makes it well suited to generic in-house solvers.

In this study the multi-group data are generated with the Monte Carlo code Serpent~2 \cite{Serpent_code_description,Serpent_homog_technique}, and the deterministic solver is developed in FreeFEM++ \cite{FreeFem_code_description_article}.

\section{Westinghouse LFR simplified model}

\section{Methodology}

The study is carried by a Python package whose main task is to manage the various data types and assemble the desired dataset for the deterministic solver. The workflow runs as follows. Serpent~2 simulations of the full-core and mini-core models produce the multi-group data. An input file then selects a subset of regions of the ``non-multiplying'' family, whose data are corrected with the SPH coefficients described below. The dataset, in any combination of corrected and uncorrected data, feeds the deterministic solver developed in FreeFEM++, and the output is compared with the reference values of the full-core Monte Carlo simulations. The kinetic parameters $\beta_{\rm eff}$ and $\Lambda_{\rm eff}$ are computed with double-weighting formulations \cite{SPH_book_Bell_Glasstone}, so the adjoint function is calculated as well.

The literature offers little on the calculation of kinetic parameters under SPH correction. To the best of the author's knowledge, the studies that address the point apply the ``non-fundamental'' SPH method, in which the correction is performed in systems with vacuum boundary conditions \cite{takasugi_2023_SPH_kinetic_parameters,INL_report_SPH_muller,Ortensi2021} -- which is not the case treated here.

\subsection{Serpent models for multi-group nuclear data generation}

Full-core Monte Carlo simulations capture the spatial flux gradients that enter the group constants. They struggle, however, to accumulate sufficient statistics in the regions far from the core, and they are computationally intensive when several core configurations must be evaluated, as in transient analyses. The default approach therefore remains a set of assembly-level systems, called ``mini-cores'' in this work. The mini-core models used here belong to three families:
%
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Model for non-peripheral fuel assemblies}, for the Fuel Assemblies (FAs) of the inner rings of the core: a single 3D fuel assembly with reflective boundary conditions in the radial direction and vacuum boundary conditions in the axial one (Figure~\ref{sph:fig:Serpent_mini_core_non_peripherical_fuel_100_geom1}).

    \item \textit{Model for non-multiplying assemblies}: a 2D model with the non-multiplying element (CR/SR, upper or lower reflector, coolant, shielding) at the center, surrounded by fuel assemblies, with reflective radial boundary conditions (Figure~\ref{sph:fig:Serpent_mini_core_non_multiplying_region_CR}). In the full-core model the CRs face two different FA types, so two variants are used. The simpler one surrounds the CR with a single FA type, generally the most numerous in the actual pattern; the second, tested in this work, reproduces the exact pattern of the FAs around the CR. A 3D model with axial vacuum boundary conditions could be employed, but the 2D choice is supported by earlier studies, which obtain accurate results with axial effects neglected -- in particular when the control rods are either fully inserted or fully withdrawn, as in the present study \cite{Nikitin_mini_core_models}.

    \item \textit{Model for peripheral fuel assemblies}, for the FAs of the external ring of the core: a 3D mini-core made of the peripheral multiplying assemblies surrounded by the radial reflector (coolant) and by the shielding, with vacuum boundary conditions both radially and axially (Figure~\ref{sph:fig:Serpent_mini_core_peripherical_fuel_400_geom1}).
\end{enumerate}

The families must then be tailored to the design at hand. For the Westinghouse LFR system the mini-core models are:

\begin{itemize}

    \item one mini-core of the ``non-peripheral fuel'' type for each of the four fuel assembly regions: Central Fuel (CF), Inner Fuel (IF), Middle Fuel (MF), Outer Fuel (OF) (Figure~\ref{sph:fig:Serpent_mini_core_non_peripherical_fuel_100_geom1});

    \item one mini-core model for each non-multiplying material region of the system: first ring of CRs, second ring of CRs -- both rodded and unrodded -- shield, radial reflector, axial reflector (Figure~\ref{sph:fig:Serpent_mini_core_non_multiplying_region_CR});

    \item one mini-core of the ``peripheral fuel'' type for the outermost ring of fuel assemblies, labeled External Fuel (EF) (Figure~\ref{sph:fig:Serpent_mini_core_peripherical_fuel_400_geom1}).

\end{itemize}

Twelve Serpent models must be simulated in total. With the same number of active and inactive cycles and particles per cycle as the full-core case, each mini-core runs faster, though not always by a wide margin, and the overall time of the mini-core option is higher. The balance shifts when more than one core configuration is considered -- a change of CR positions, a different fuel arrangement: a new full-core MC simulation is needed for every configuration, so the full-core route quickly becomes inefficient \cite{SPH_computational_time}. The mini-core route has its own weak point: whenever a distributed variable changes, such as the temperature field or the depletion stage of the system, all the mini-core models must be launched again to regenerate the multi-group data for the new distribution.

\subsection{SPH algorithm for the deterministic solver}
The goal of the SPH method is to preserve the reaction rates in the selected regions of the system used to generate the SPH coefficients. To this end, a supercell model is reproduced in the deterministic code as in the reference simulation, the regions are homogenized, and the eigenvalue problem is solved iteratively with corrected data until the difference between the new SPH coefficients and the previous ones falls below a fixed tolerance. The SPH equations read:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{x,r,g}^{\text{ref}} = \tau_{x,r,g}^{\text{FF}} = \Sigma_{x,r,g}^{*} \, \phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}} = \mu_{r,g} \, \Sigma_{x,r,g}^{\text{ref}} \, \phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}} = N_{g} \frac{\phi_{r,g}^{\text{ref}}}{\phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}}}  \Sigma_{x,r,g}^{\text{ref}} \, \phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_r V_r \, \phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}}}{\displaystyle\sum_r V_r \, \phi_{r,g}^{\text{ref}}} \frac{\phi_{r,g}^{\text{ref}}}{\phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}}}  \Sigma_{x,r,g}^{\text{ref}} \, \phi_{r,g}^{\text{FF}},
\label{sph:eq:SPH_equations}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ denotes the reaction rates, $\Sigma_{x,r,g}$ a generic cross section of region $r$ and group $g$, $\phi_{r,g}$ the neutron flux in group $g$ and region $r$, $V_r$ the volume (or area) of the region, and $\mu_{r,g}$ the correction factor. The SPH method is formulated as a fixed-source problem, and with no constraint on the SPH factors it admits infinitely many solutions. A normalization condition makes the set unique: in the original SPH approach, the flux averaged over the entire geometry of the supercell is preserved through the equivalence procedure, and the normalization factor $N_g$ is defined as the ratio of the sums of the spectra over the regions \cite{Ortensi_normalization_factor}. The superscripts ``ref'' and ``FF'' refer to the Serpent reference results and to the homogenized FreeFEM++ model, respectively. The tuned multi-group data are $\Sigma_{t,g}$, $D_{g}$, $\Sigma_{g' \to g}$ and $\nu\Sigma_{f,g}$. For the scattering matrix, the production matrix is used: neglecting $(n,xn)$ reactions would systematically underestimate the neutron production and lower the reactivity.
%
Following Ref.~\cite{Nikitin_mini_core_models}, only non-multiplying regions, such as the CRs and the empty CR channels, undergo the SPH procedure, sketched in Figure~\ref{sph:fig:SPH_flowchart}. Figure~\ref{sph:fig:SPH_convergence_example} shows an example of the convergence of the SPH coefficients and of the mini-core $k_{\rm eff}$.

\subsection{Full-core FreeFEM++ solver}

The influence of the MGXS generated from the full-core and mini-core models is assessed with a deterministic three-dimensional neutron diffusion solver developed in FreeFEM++. The FEM was chosen because the tool is meant to serve not only the LFR design considered here but also the highly compact microreactor concepts under development at Westinghouse, such as eVinci: complex geometries and unconventional core layouts, where the FEM offers more flexibility and accuracy than traditional nodal methods.
The models are considered with and without SPH (superhomogenization) corrections. The homogenization is assembly-wise: each fuel assembly is one homogenized region, which keeps the model simple while preserving accuracy at the core level. The high radial symmetry of the core, together with some geometric simplifications in the axial direction, where the non-active materials are removed, allows representing half of the axial geometry only; the modeled geometry is thereby reduced by a factor of 24 with respect to the full physical system. Figure~\ref{sph:fig:FF_fullcore_model_geom_mesh} shows the geometry together with the mesh used for every full-core calculation. The mesh, of prismatic elements, was generated with the open-source software \texttt{Gmsh} \cite{geuzaine2009gmsh}. No dedicated mesh optimization study was performed, as it lies beyond the scope of this work; grid independence was nonetheless verified by adopting a sufficiently fine mesh, affordable thanks to the low computational cost of the deterministic model.

\centering

FreeFEM++ requires the user to define explicitly the weak formulation of the governing equations, so the neutron diffusion equation must be recast in variational form. The result is:
%
\begin{align}
\int_{\Omega} \nabla \left( D_g(\vec{r}) \, \phi_g(\vec{r}) \, w_g \right) \, d\vec{r}
+ \int_{\Omega} \Sigma_{t,g}(\vec{r}) \, \phi_g(\vec{r}) \, w_g \, d\vec{r}
= \frac{1}{k_{\text{eff}}} \int_{\Omega} \chi_g(\vec{r}) \, w_g \sum_{g'=1}^{G} \nu \Sigma_{f,g'}(\vec{r}) \, \phi_{g'}(\vec{r}) \, d\vec{r} \notag \\
+ \int_{\Omega} w_g \sum_{g'=1}^{G} \Sigma_{g' \rightarrow g}(\vec{r}) \, \phi_{g'}(\vec{r}) \, d\vec{r} + \int_{\Omega} \nabla(w_g \nabla \phi_g) d\vec{r}
\label{sph:eq:diffusion_weak_formulation}
\end{align}
%
where the $w_g$ are the test functions of each energy group. By the Gauss--Green theorem, the last term on the right-hand side becomes:
%
\begin{equation}
    \int_{\Omega} \nabla(w_g \nabla \phi_g) d\vec{r} = \int_{\partial \Omega_v}  w_g \nabla \phi_g \cdot \hat{n}dS
    \label{sph:eq:bc_term_of_weak_formulation_green_theo}
\end{equation}

The P$_1$ angular treatment then defines the outgoing current at the vacuum boundaries, turning Eq.~(\ref{sph:eq:bc_term_of_weak_formulation_green_theo}) into:
%
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\partial \Omega_v}  w_g \nabla \phi_g \cdot \hat{n}dS = - \int_{\partial \Omega_v}  \frac{1}{2}w_g \phi_g dS
    \label{sph:eq:bc_term_of_weak_formulation_P1}
\end{equation}
%
The neutron diffusion equation is then solved for each energy group, one dataset at a time, combining SPH-corrected and uncorrected MGXS in the different assembly types of the core.

The kinetic parameters computed are the effective delayed neutron fraction $\beta_{\rm eff}$ and the effective prompt neutron generation time $\Lambda_{\rm eff}$:
\begin{equation}
\beta_{\rm eff} = \frac{\langle\phi^{\dagger}|\hat{F}_d\phi\rangle}{\langle\phi^{\dagger}|\hat{F}_t\phi\rangle}
\label{sph:eq:beta_eff}
\end{equation}
%
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_{\rm eff} = \frac{\left\langle\phi^{\dagger}|\frac{1}{v}\phi\right\rangle}{\langle\phi^{\dagger}|\hat{F}_t\phi\rangle}.
\label{sph:eq:lambda_eff}
\end{equation}
Their evaluation requires the adjoint flux $\phi^{\dagger}$, so the neutronic problem is solved twice: the adjoint solution is obtained by transposing the operators on the right-hand side of the neutron diffusion equation, leaving the left-hand side untouched. The adjoint problem uses the uncorrected MGXS. Previous studies, such as Ref.~\cite{takasugi_2023_SPH_kinetic_parameters}, show that applying the SPH method to the adjoint problem is not always advantageous: restricting SPH to the fuel region in the forward calculation improves the preservation of the kinetic parameters, while extending it to the adjoint $k$-eigenvalue calculation introduces inconsistencies in their bilinear weighting and degrades the reliability of the results. For the adjoint problem, SPH is therefore best avoided, or applied with great caution.

replacing the CR MGC with the mini-core data and applying the SPH factors brings them to 43 pcm and 0.23\%. The mini-core dataset deserves a remark: it agrees well on the multiplication eigenvalue, yet it gives the worst prediction of the distributed quantity, with the highest RMSE, 0.42\%. In general $k_{\rm eff}$ is more prone to hide error compensation than a distributed quantity such as the power density.

Across both configurations, the full-core dataset yields the least accurate $k_{\rm eff}$, and every case that uses the detailed CR description with corrections on the CR and SFA data yields the most accurate one. The reason lies in the construction of the method: SPH forces the reaction rates of the corrected regions to match those of the reference mini-core model, so if that reference represents well the flux distribution of the corresponding region in the full-core calculation, the corrected solution follows it closely. The deterministic model without SPH correction, by contrast, shows noticeable deviations, likely due to the limited number of energy groups and/or of universes defined in Serpent~2 for the MGXS generation. All comparisons, however, were performed under consistent modeling conditions, which keeps the observations meaningful within the scope of this study. One further advantage of the SPH method emerges here: it mitigates the dependence of the deterministic results on the chosen energy group structure and on the spatial subdivision used for collapsing the data -- an attractive feature wherever computational efficiency and accuracy must be balanced.

As expected, $\beta_{\rm eff}$ and $\Lambda_{\rm eff}$ draw no benefit from the SPH correction. Unlike the reaction rates, which the SPH equivalence preserves by construction, nothing guarantees that the kinetic parameters of Serpent are preserved in the deterministic calculation: collapsing them with a bilinear weighting built on the forward and adjoint multi-group fluxes of the deterministic code does not ensure the preservation of the reference values. If the reaction rates are preserved, the bilinear reaction rate cannot be preserved at the same time. The SPH equivalence theory preserves reaction rates by modifying cross sections; for bilinear quantities such as the kinetic parameters a theoretical gap remains, all the more when the correction touches only some sub-regions of the core, as here.

It would not be accurate, though, to call the two parameters insensitive to the corrections: Figure~\ref{sph:fig:kinetic_parameters_ARI_and_ARO_results} shows variations across the datasets. For $\beta_{\rm eff}$ the changes are minor, as its definition explains: the numerator of Eq.~\ref{sph:eq:beta_eff} carries the delayed fission operator $\hat{F}_d = \beta \chi_d \nu \Sigma_f$, which vanishes in non-fissile regions such as the CR assemblies, precisely where the correction acts. The small differences that do appear are second-order: the SPH correction modifies the cross sections of the CR regions, which perturbs the local neutron flux near the CR assemblies, and the perturbation propagates weakly to $\beta_{\rm eff}$.

$\Lambda_{\rm eff}$ behaves differently. In the ARI case it is consistently underestimated when the SPH corrections are applied and overestimated when they are not. The numerator of Eq.~\ref{sph:eq:lambda_eff} never vanishes, since $\frac{1}{v}$ is always finite, and it varies through the neutron flux $\phi$, which responds to the SPH factors; the denominator does not change, because the corrections act only on the CR regions, where $\Sigma_f = 0$. In the ARO configuration $\Lambda_{\rm eff}$ stays nearly constant across the first datasets -- the flux barely changes, as Figure~\ref{sph:fig:ARO_power_density_distribution_4_cases} confirms -- and drops visibly for the last two. There the correction extends beyond the CR assemblies to the surrounding fuel assemblies, so the denominator picks up non-zero fission terms and grows, and the parameter decreases.

Table~\ref{sph:tab:all_results_table} gathers all the results discussed so far, together with the maximum power density relative error ($E_p$) and the computational time needed to generate the multi-group data. Building the dataset from mini-core models costs more time. Their advantage lies elsewhere: since they are defined independently of the surrounding assembly pattern (with the exception of the non-multiplying type), no new full Monte Carlo simulation is needed when the core configuration changes -- a CR repositioning, a fuel reshuffling. The advantage fades when several distributed parameters, such as temperatures, must be covered: every parameter value requires regenerating the whole dataset.

Among the configurations tested, the cheapest is the ``full-core'' dataset, followed by ``full-core + CR SPH'', which adds only two mini-core models for the CR MGC to the full-core run. All the remaining cases rest on the complete set of mini-core models, about 70 hours of Monte Carlo calculations, performed on a PC with an Intel\textsuperscript{\textregistered} Xeon\textsuperscript{\textregistered} Gold 6130 CPU @ 2.1\,GHz using 30 cores.

As discussed above, ``mini-core + CR detailed + CR and SFA SPH'' is the most accurate configuration in both cases. Balancing accuracy against computational cost, ``full-core + CR SPH'' emerges as the most favorable option overall.

\section{Conclusions}
This chapter investigated the possibility of reproducing the Serpent~2 reference results for the Westinghouse LFR core design with a finite-element code solving the neutron diffusion equation. Several routes for generating the multi-group constants were explored: a standard full-core model, and a set of mini-core models representative of the different regions of the system. The mini-core route pays off, in accuracy and in computational time, whenever several core configurations -- control rod positions, for instance -- must be analyzed: a full-core model would have to be rerun for each of them, at a significant cost if low statistical errors are wanted in the non-multiplying regions or in materials far from the core, such as the shielding. The advantage does not carry over to parametric studies over distributed variables. When multi-group libraries must cover several temperatures and burnup levels, the whole set of mini-core simulations -- about 70 hours in this study, with the same neutron population settings in Serpent~2 -- must be repeated for every temperature and every burnup step, a total effort scaling as $N_{\text{temperature}} \times N_{\text{burnup}}$. Large parametric studies then become impractical, unless a highly reliable deterministic code takes over the data generation: one able to reproduce the neutron spectrum of the system accurately and to extract the information needed for the SPH correction coefficients.
A caveat on the timing figures: using the same number of particles and cycles for the full-core and the mini-core models was a deliberate choice, made to keep the comparison simple. Mini-core models generally need fewer histories, particularly for cells with reflective boundary conditions, and with cycle counts tailored to each model -- at comparable cross-section accuracy -- the total computational time drops to approximately 48 hours for the ARI configuration and 53 hours for the ARO one.

The chapter also showed how the SPH method can tune the multi-group data to improve the reference quantities, using $k_{\rm eff}$, $\beta_{\rm eff}$, $\Lambda_{\rm eff}$ and the power density distribution for the comparison. The time taken by the SPH-correction algorithm itself is negligible, of the order of seconds per supercell.

With the SPH correction applied, the power density distribution and $k_{\rm eff}$ of the deterministic model agree well with the reference. In the ARO configuration the simulation based on the mini-core dataset is less sensitive to the correction, because the system is less heterogeneous. The models relying on the full-core dataset produce the poorest results overall. Fast reactors are, in fact, favorable ground for the SPH method: the relatively uniform neutron flux, the reduced anisotropy and the smoother energy dependence of the cross sections limit the multi-group collapsing errors and let scalar correction factors preserve the global quantities with high fidelity.

The values of $\beta_{\rm eff}$ and $\Lambda_{\rm eff}$ were obtained with the double-weighting formulation. They respond to the cross-section correction, but without any direct improvement: the SPH method preserves reaction rates of the form $\Sigma_x \phi$, a product of a macroscopic cross section and the neutron flux, not bilinear quantities weighted with the adjoint function.

Looking ahead, this line of work will keep building on the mini-core route to multi-group data generation, in particular for uncertainty and sensitivity analyses related to nuclear data in transient calculations, and will aim at improving the reproduction of the kinetic parameters under SPH correction.
