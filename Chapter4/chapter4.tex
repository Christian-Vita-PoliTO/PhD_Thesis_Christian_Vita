\chapter{Neural Network-Assisted Optimization of the LFR Multi-Group Energy Grid Driven by Genetic Algorithms}\label{ch:energy-grid}

\section{Introduction}

Full-core deterministic simulations of fast reactors, and in particular the
transient analyses for which they are ultimately intended, are performed on a
condensed energy grid. The number of groups and the placement of their
boundaries determine how faithfully the condensed calculation reproduces a
Monte Carlo reference, and the dependence is neither smooth nor intuitive, since
two structures with the same number of groups can differ by orders of magnitude
in accuracy. The impact of energy collapsing on the integral parameters of a
fast system has been analysed in detail for the neutron
lifetime~\cite{collapsing}, and the identification of the structure itself has
been addressed with several metaheuristics: particle swarm optimization for
thermal lattices~\cite{psoThermal}, genetic algorithms for multi-group searches
in SIMMER~\cite{massoneGA,simmerGA} and for the ARC fusion
reactor~\cite{arcGA}, simulated annealing for sodium-cooled fast
reactors~\cite{sfrSA}, hierarchical division~\cite{hierDiv}, and dedicated
studies for specific systems~\cite{scwr}.

For the lead-cooled fast reactor the subject has been developed through a
consistent series of works~\citetext{\citealp{lfrGA2022}; \citealp{lfrGA2024}; \citealp{prevGA}},
culminating in the multi-configuration optimization problem of
\citet{prevMulti}, in which a single energy structure is optimized
simultaneously for three control-rod configurations, with the goal of having
an adequate grid as the core evolves during a transient. That study reports an observation which motivates the
present one. In that work, the genetic algorithm is run from three different random seeds,
referred to as tribes, and the three tribes converge to three different
structures sharing many boundaries but not all of them. Since the tribes differ
only in the initial population, the outcome indicates that the objective
possesses several distinct optima of comparable quality, and that a single run
samples one of them without any indication of which.

The behaviour has an intuitive explanation in the nature of the problem. The
decision variable is a subset of the internal boundaries of a fine grid, so that
the search space is combinatorial rather than continuous and contains
$2^{29}\approx5.4\times10^{8}$ elements, on which no notion of gradient exists.
The objective is the product of fifteen terms, which measure, in each of the
three control-rod configurations, the agreement of five quantities with the
Monte Carlo reference: the multiplication factor, the effective delayed neutron
fraction, the effective neutron lifetime and the distributions of power and of
flux over the core. The terms do not respond in the same way to a change of the
structure and can move in opposite directions: a change that reduces the
deviation on the multiplication factor may increase the deviation on the flux
distribution. The resulting surface is expected to possess many local optima
separated by barriers that a
population-based search crosses or does not cross depending on where it starts,
which is precisely what three tribes with three different outcomes indicate.

Two consequences follow. The first is that the result of a single optimization
should not be interpreted as the optimal structure, but as a nearly optimal solution, i.e., one member of a set
whose extent is unknown. The second is that any statement of general validity
about where the boundaries of a good structure should lie requires many
independent searches, not only one. Both consequences call for the same remedy, i.e., a higher
number of simulated tribes, to provide a better characterization of an optimized
energy grid.

That remedy is not affordable within the workflow shown in \citet{prevMulti}. The fitness of a
candidate structure can only be established by running the full-core
model, and a population-based search requires one such evaluation for
every individual of every generation. In the present case a single evaluation
covering the three control-rod configurations costs on average $46$~s on three cores with
the neutronics module of FRENETIC~\cite{frenetic,freneticBench}, which solves the
multi-group diffusion equations with a nodal method. Hence, one
search of a hundred individuals over a hundred generations amounts to
approximately $383$ core-hours. One thousand similar searches would exceed
$380\,000$ core-hours, which places the analysis out of reach.

The present chapter aims at removing this bottleneck by replacing the evaluation inside the
search with a neural network model of the fitness functions, while leaving
FRENETIC in charge of the conclusions. The metamodel is used to explore,
to rank, and to propose; every structure that the searches produce is then
recomputed with FRENETIC, and all the quantities discussed in the following
sections are those returned by FRENETIC. The metamodel is therefore an
accelerator of the search and not a substitute for the physical model, an arrangement
which also protects the results from the error of the metamodel.

The chapter first defines the optimization problem, reporting in full the
chromosome representation and the fitness functions inherited from
\citet{prevMulti}. It then describes the metamodel, its training and its
accuracy. The resulting workflow, summarized in Fig.~\tref{eg:fig:flow}, is presented next,
followed by the analysis of the thousand searches, of the structures they
produce, and of the features those structures have in common.

\section{Definition of the Optimization Problem}

The case study is the ALFRED core, in the specification of the lead-cooled fast
reactor benchmark coordinated by the Nuclear Energy Agency~\cite{neaBench}.
Three control-rod configurations are considered simultaneously, shown in
Fig.~\tref{eg:fig:core} together with the radial layout of the core: rods fully
withdrawn, with the absorber entirely below the active fuel; rods partially
inserted, with the absorber facing the fuel over a length of $28$~cm; and rods
fully inserted, with the absorber covering the whole active height. The
multiplication factor of the Monte Carlo reference calculation falls from
$1.096$ to $1.072$ and then to $1.019$ across the three, and they are denoted in
what follows by the subscripts ``out'', ``partial'' and ``in'', respectively.

The structure is optimized with a genetic algorithm, the method adopted in the
previous works on the same
problem~\citetext{\citealp{lfrGA2022}; \citealp{lfrGA2024}; \citealp{prevGA}; \citealp{prevMulti}}.
Genetic algorithms solve an optimization problem by emulating the mechanisms of
biological evolution, and have been in use since the 1970s~\citep{holland1975}.
They belong to the meta-heuristics: compared with a rigorous optimization
algorithm, they do not guarantee the global optimum, but they yield a solution
close to it while exploring only a limited part of the solution space. Each
candidate solution, or individual, is encoded as a chromosome, a sequence of
genes whose values, called alleles, determine the solution it represents;
decoding a chromosome yields that solution, here a group structure. The search
starts from a random initial population, and every individual is judged by a
fitness function, which in this problem expresses how closely the condensed
full-core calculation reproduces the Monte Carlo reference. The fitness
function implements the principle of natural selection: the best individuals
have more chances of being selected for breeding, in which crossover recombines
portions of the chromosomes of two parents into new individuals; the best
individuals of a generation can also pass unchanged into the next one, which is
called elitism, and mutation changes a few genes of the new individuals at
random. Generation after generation, the average quality of the population tends
to improve, since desirable features are inherited while disadvantageous ones do
not last. The balance between the exploration of new regions of the solution
space and the refinement of the solutions already found is governed by the
selection pressure, that is, the advantage given to the individuals of better
fitness, and by the rates of crossover and mutation. The process is repeated for
a prescribed number of generations. The method needs only the value of the
fitness function of each individual, with no derivative and no assumption on its
regularity, such as convexity, which suits a problem whose variables are
discrete and whose objective is evaluated by an external code. The problem also
lends itself to the genetic representation: a group structure is a sequence of
energy boundaries, each strongly influenced by its neighbours, much as a
chromosome is a sequence of genes~\citep{prevGA}. Since the initial population
is random and the operators make random choices, the outcome is a solution close
to the optimum that depends on the random seed, and searches started from
different seeds can converge to different solutions. The two ingredients
specific to the problem, the fitness functions and the chromosome, are defined
in the following.

\subsection{Reference Data and Fitness Functions}

Serpent~2 is used to generate, for each core configuration, both the reference
values of the integral parameters and the spatially homogenized fine-group
constants, evaluated at isothermal conditions with fuel and coolant at $673$~K.
The data are collapsed onto a fine structure of 30 groups whose boundaries are
extracted from the ECCO-33 grid, chosen so that few groups describe the thermal
and the fast regions, where the statistical uncertainty of the Monte Carlo
calculation is largest. The 30-group structure constitutes the pool of
boundaries available to the optimization.

For a candidate structure $I$, the agreement with the Monte Carlo reference is
measured by five fitness functions per configuration~\cite{prevMulti}. Three of
them concern integral quantities,
%
\begin{equation}
  f_k^I = \left|k^I - k^\mathrm{MC}\right|\times10^5, \qquad
  f_{\beta}^I = \left|\beta_\mathrm{eff}^I - \beta_\mathrm{eff}^\mathrm{MC}\right|
  \times10^5, \qquad
  f_{\Lambda}^I = \left|\frac{\Lambda_\mathrm{eff}^I -
  \Lambda_\mathrm{eff}^\mathrm{MC}}{\Lambda_\mathrm{eff}^\mathrm{MC}}\right|\times10^2,
  \label{eg:eqn:integral}
\end{equation}
%
namely the multiplication factor, the effective delayed neutron fraction and
the effective neutron lifetime. The last is the adjoint-weighted mean lifetime
that the reference calculation tallies, written here $\Lambda_\mathrm{eff}$
following the works this chapter builds on; it is not the neutron generation
time, from which it differs by the multiplication factor. The remaining two concern the
distributions over the $J$ core assemblies,
%
\begin{equation}
  f_P^I = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\frac{P_j^I-P_j^\mathrm{MC}}
  {P_j^\mathrm{MC}}\right)^{2}}, \qquad
  f_{\Phi}^I = \sqrt{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{j=1}^{J} w_{g,j}
  \left(\frac{\Phi_{g,j}^I-\Phi_{g,j}^\mathrm{MC}}
  {\Phi_{g,j}^\mathrm{MC}}\right)^{2}},
  \label{eg:eqn:distributed}
\end{equation}
%
the flux deviation of each group and assembly being weighted by the share of the
total importance of the core that it carries,
%
\begin{equation}
  w_{g,j} = \frac{\sum_{m\in g} n_{m,j}\,\phi^{\dagger}_{m,j}\,V_j}
                 {\sum_{m=1}^{H}\sum_{j'=1}^{J} n_{m,j'}\,\phi^{\dagger}_{m,j'}\,V_{j'}},
  \label{eg:eqn:weight}
\end{equation}
%
where $m$ runs over the $H=30$ groups of the fine structure, $m\in g$ denoting
those contained in group $g$ of the candidate, $V_j$ is the volume of assembly
$j$, and $n=\phi/v$ and $\phi^{\dagger}$ are the neutron density and the adjoint
flux of a FRENETIC calculation performed on the fine structure, the speed $v$ of
each group being the one tabulated in the reference library. The product
$n\phi^{\dagger}=\phi\phi^{\dagger}/v$ is the total importance, which measures how
much the neutrons of a group contribute to the chain reaction; its distribution
over the groups of the optimized structures is examined further on. All
five are deviations from the reference, so that smaller values indicate a better
structure.

Since the five quantities differ by orders of magnitude and in range of
variability, each is mapped onto the interval $[1,100]$ by the linear scaling
%
\begin{equation}
  h_L : f \longrightarrow 1 + 99\,
  \frac{f-\min_{\forall I\in E}\left(f^I\right)}
       {\max_{\forall I\in E}\left(f^I\right)-\min_{\forall I\in E}\left(f^I\right)},
  \label{eg:eqn:scaling}
\end{equation}
%
where $E$ denotes the set of solutions evaluated so far, and the joint fitness
of a candidate is the product of the fifteen scaled terms, five for each of the
three configurations,
%
\begin{equation}
  FF^I = \prod_{c\in\{\mathrm{out},\,\mathrm{partial},\,\mathrm{in}\}}
  h_L\!\left(f_{k,c}^I\right) h_L\!\left(f_{P,c}^I\right)
  h_L\!\left(f_{\Phi,c}^I\right) h_L\!\left(f_{\Lambda,c}^I\right)
  h_L\!\left(f_{\beta,c}^I\right).
  \label{eg:eqn:joint}
\end{equation}
%
Before the scaling, the three integral fitness functions are clipped from below
and the flux fitness function is replaced by its base-ten logarithm floored at
unity. The threshold of the clipping is $10$~pcm, and since
Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:integral}) does not express the three functions in the same
units it takes a different numerical value in each. For $f_k$ and $f_{\beta}$,
defined as absolute deviations multiplied by $10^{5}$, the threshold
corresponds to $f_k = 10$ and $f_{\beta} = 10$; for $f_{\Lambda}$, defined as a
relative deviation expressed as a percentage, the same threshold corresponds to
$f_{\Lambda} = 0.01$. For $f_k$ and $f_{\beta}$ the threshold
stands a few times above the statistical uncertainty of the reference, which
is below $2$~pcm on the multiplication factor and below $1$~pcm on the delayed
neutron fraction; for $f_{\Lambda}$ it stands below it, since the reference
lifetime carries an uncertainty of about $0.05\%$, so that
a structure has to reproduce the lifetime more finely than the reference
resolves it before the control takes effect. Its purpose is to mark
the level below which a difference between two structures is not treated as an
improvement: a quantity reproduced to within the threshold is clipped and stops
contributing to the ranking, so that the search is directed towards what is
still worth improving, and a term already at the precision of the reference
cannot dominate the product. The logarithmic floor on the flux acts instead as a
compression of scale. All of them are applied identically to the reference
values and to the candidate ones.

\subsection{Chromosome Representation}

The optimization variable is the set of internal boundaries retained from the
fine structure. Of the 31 boundaries of the 30-group grid the two extremes are
fixed, so that a candidate structure corresponds to a subset of the remaining
$H-1=29$ internal boundaries, and the design space, that is, the set of all the
subsets of these boundaries, contains $2^{29}\approx5.4\times10^{8}$ elements.

The earlier formulations of this problem fixed the number of groups before the 
search and left the genetic algorithm to place the boundaries alone, with one
binary gene per internal boundary of the fine grid stating whether it is
retained. Prescribing $G$ restricts the space that can be reached and excludes
the structures that a different number of groups would make available. Several
chromosome definitions were compared in previous works, and the one retained combines the features of the others and carries
the number of groups as a gene of its own, so that $G$ is searched over rather
than imposed~\cite{prevGA}.

The representation adopted here is that polyploid encoding, in the form used in
the multi-configuration formulation of \citet{prevMulti}, reproduced in full because it determines both the
behaviour of the genetic operators and the input of the metamodel. A chromosome
consists of two sets. The first, $\gamma_0$, is a single gene holding the number
of groups $G$ of the structure, constrained to $4\le G\le20$. The second,
$\gamma_1$, is polyploid and contains $P=4$ arrays of $H-1=29$ genes each, one
gene per internal boundary, whose alleles take integer values in $\{0,1,2\}$. An
individual is therefore described by $1+P(H-1)=117$ variables.

Decoding proceeds in two steps. For each internal boundary $i$ the $P$ arrays
are summed into a score
%
\begin{equation}
  s_i = \sum_{p=1}^{P} \gamma_{1,p,i}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,H-1,
  \label{eg:eqn:score}
\end{equation}
%
and the $G-1$ boundaries with the highest score are retained; when several boundaries have the same score, those with the smaller index $i$
are retained first. The polyploid structure gives each boundary
a graded rather than binary expression, so that a boundary can be strengthened
or weakened by mutation without being switched on or off abruptly, which
smooths the effect of the genetic operators on the decoded structure. A repair
operator enforces the bounds on $G$ and the validity of the alleles after
crossover and mutation. Figure~\tref{eg:fig:chromosome} illustrates the
encoding and its decoding with an example.

\section{The Metamodel}

\subsection{Architecture} %CV: non pensi che qua sarebbe necessario uno schema?

The fifteen fitness functions are not independent: they are of five types, one
for each physical quantity (multiplication factor, effective delayed neutron
fraction, effective neutron lifetime, power and flux), and each type is
evaluated for the same three control-rod configurations. The metamodel
reproduces that organization with a feed-forward neural network, in which the
input passes through a sequence of intermediate, or hidden, layers of neurons
before reaching the outputs. The layers are arranged in three levels. The
first level, called the trunk because the other levels branch off from it, is
common to all the outputs. It receives the description of the structure as 29
bits, one for each internal boundary of the fine grid and equal to one when the
boundary is retained, together with the number of groups, and it consists of
three fully connected layers of $1024$, $512$ and $256$ neurons, in which every
neuron receives the outputs of all the neurons of the preceding layer. The
trunk feeds five type branches, one for each type of fitness function, each made
of two layers of $128$ and $64$ neurons. Each type branch in turn feeds three
configuration branches, one for each control-rod configuration, each made of a
layer of $32$ neurons terminating in the corresponding output. Every hidden
layer uses the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function, $\max(0,x)$,
and each configuration branch in fact ends in two numbers, the predicted value
and the logarithm of a variance, whose only role is in the loss function
described further on. The parameters of the trunk are thus shared by all the
outputs, while those of the branches are specific to one type of fitness
function or to one configuration.

Twelve of the fifteen outputs span several orders of magnitude and are learned
as $\log_{10}(y+10^{-6})$; the offset $10^{-6}$, smaller than any value in the
training data, only keeps the transformation defined for a vanishing deviation.
The three power fitness functions are instead learned as they are, without the
logarithmic transformation. The prediction of the metamodel is the average of
the predictions of an ensemble~\cite{deepEnsembles}, that is, a set of five
networks with the same architecture, trained on the same data from different
random initializations of their weights. The average is taken in the space in
which the networks are trained, which for the twelve logarithmic outputs
amounts to a geometric mean in physical units. The disagreement among the
members of the ensemble indicates how uncertain the prediction is, and the
construction of the training set rests on it.

\subsection{Training Set}

The metamodel is trained on more than $90\,000$ distinct structures, for each of
which the neutronics module of FRENETIC has been run over the three
configurations. Since every structure added to the training set costs a
full-core calculation, the training set is built by adaptive sampling, so that
each addition is as informative as possible: an initial set is drawn at random
from all the candidate structures, and the structures added afterwards are
those, among the candidates for which FRENETIC has not yet been run, on which
the members of an ensemble of networks disagree most, that is, those for which
the prediction of the metamodel is most uncertain.

A further 1\,700 structures form an external validation set, which is used
neither in training nor for early stopping, the procedure that ends the
training when the error on a separate set of structures, kept aside for this
purpose, stops decreasing. The external validation set nevertheless guides the
development: the architectures and trunk widths explored in the preliminary
phase are compared on their accuracy on this set, overall and output by output.
The accuracy figures quoted in this work, which all refer to this set, may
therefore be slightly optimistic. The conclusions of this chapter do not depend
on them, however, because the workflow described later verifies with FRENETIC
every result it uses. The ablation and reproducibility measurements reported
further on are also evaluated on this set, after the architecture has been
fixed.

\subsection{Training and Accuracy}

The inputs, namely the 29 bits of the structure and the number of groups, and
the fifteen targets, in the space defined above, logarithmic for all but the
three power functions, are standardized to
zero mean and unit variance, with the mean and the standard deviation of each
variable computed on the training portion alone. An internal validation split,
$15\%$ of the training structures drawn once with a fixed seed, serves for early
stopping; it is distinct from the external validation set described above.

The loss is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood built from the two outputs of
each branch, averaged over the mini-batch, the subset of training structures
used for a single update of the network weights,
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} \;=\; \frac{1}{15}\sum_{q=1}^{15} \frac{1}{2}
  \left[\, s_q + \frac{\left(\mu_q - t_q\right)^2}{e^{\,s_q}} \right],
  \label{eg:eqn:nll}
\end{equation}
%
with $\mu_q$ the predicted value of output $q$, $t_q$ its target and $s_q$ the
predicted log-variance, clamped to $|s_q|\le5$. The predicted variance acts as a
self-adjusting weight on the residuals during training and appears nowhere
else: predictions, accuracy figures and the selection of the structures added by
adaptive sampling use the means alone.

The networks are trained in \textit{PyTorch}~\cite{pytorch} with the AdamW
optimizer~\cite{adamw}, a gradient-based method that adapts the step of each
weight during training, used here without weight decay, that is, without the
penalty on large weights that the method can include. The training structures
are processed in mini-batches of 200, reshuffled at every epoch, that is, at
every pass over the training set, and the training proceeds in four stages of
decreasing learning rate, the base step of the optimizer, $3\times10^{-3}$,
$3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-4}$ and $3\times10^{-5}$. Each stage stops when the loss on the internal
validation split has not improved for 40, 12, 12 and 10 epochs respectively,
within caps of 400, 60, 60 and 40 epochs, and restores the best weights seen;
most of the training takes place in the first stage, which lasts between 168
and 302 epochs depending on the member. Training the whole ensemble, the five members in
parallel, takes 79 minutes on CPUs alone. The staged schedule proves
considerably more effective than a constant learning rate: compared with a
constant learning rate of $3\times10^{-3}$ with early stopping, it lowers the
median relative error of an ensemble of five networks of this architecture by
about $40\%$, more than the combined effect of the architectural refinements
discussed further on.

The accuracy of the metamodel is measured on the external validation set by
comparing, for each structure and each output, the value $\hat{f}$ predicted by
the metamodel with the value $f$ computed with FRENETIC for the same structure.
Two measures are used. The relative error,
%
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \frac{\left|\hat{f}-f\right|}{f}\times10^{2},
  \label{eg:eqn:relerr}
\end{equation}
%
is computed in physical units and summarized by its median over the structures
of the set, and over the fifteen outputs as well when a single figure is given.
The coefficient of determination of an output,
%
\begin{equation}
  R^2 = 1-\frac{\sum_{n}\left(\hat{y}_n-y_n\right)^2}
               {\sum_{n}\left(y_n-\bar{y}\right)^2},
  \label{eg:eqn:r2}
\end{equation}
%
is computed instead in the space in which the networks are trained, $y_n$ being
the transformed value of the output for structure $n$, $\hat{y}_n$ its
prediction and $\bar{y}$ the mean of $y_n$ over the set. It equals one when the
predictions are exact and decreases as the residuals grow with respect to the
spread of the values; the mean coefficient of determination is its average over
the fifteen outputs. The relative error thus measures how far the metamodel is
from FRENETIC, whereas each fitness function measures how far the condensed
calculation is from the Monte Carlo reference.

Table~\tref{eg:tab:accuracy} reports the accuracy of the final ensemble for each
output and Fig.~\tref{eg:fig:scatter} the values predicted by the metamodel
against those computed with FRENETIC. The mean coefficient of determination is
$0.996$ and the median relative error $0.403\%$. The relative error is not
uniform across the fifteen outputs: it is below $0.2\%$ for the power fitness
functions, between $0.17$ and $0.40\%$ for those of the delayed neutron fraction
and between $2.0$ and $2.3\%$ for those of the flux, which are the least
accurately predicted.

\subsection{Choice of the Architecture}

The size of the network is determined empirically. Since generating the
training data is by far the most expensive part of the work, tuning the
network is the most economical action: architectures, trunk widths and training
parameters are explored extensively to improve the predictive accuracy
obtained with the same data, and the architectural parameters given in the
description of the architecture, the numbers of layers and of neurons, are the
result of that exploration.

An ablation, in which levels of the network are removed in turn, quantifies
what each level of the hierarchy contributes, and shows that the two levels of
branches contribute very differently.
Table~\tref{eg:tab:ablation} reports three architectures trained on the same
data with the same reduced protocol, a single network at constant learning rate.
Introducing the five type branches between the trunk and the outputs raises the
mean coefficient of determination from $0.9848$ to $0.9908$ and lowers the
median relative error from $1.75$ to $1.50\%$, at the price of a $29\%$
increase in the number of parameters. Adding the configuration branches below
them changes neither figure appreciably, $0.9905$ and $1.49\%$.

The comparison between the last two architectures rests on differences below
$4\times10^{-4}$ in the mean coefficient of determination and below $0.01$
percentage points in the median relative error. To establish whether differences of this size are meaningful,
the reproducibility of the training is measured: five networks of identical
architecture, trained with the identical protocol and differing only in the
random initialization of their weights, span a range of $0.0031$ in the
coefficient of determination and of $0.24$ percentage points in relative error, with
standard deviations of $0.0013$ and $0.11$. The difference between the two
architectures is thus about four times smaller than the standard deviation over
runs of the same one, and fifteen times smaller on the relative error. The comparison of
the fifteen outputs one by one leads to the same conclusion. The type branches
improve twelve outputs out of fifteen with respect to the network without them,
so the gain is shared by most of the predicted quantities. The configuration
branches improve eight outputs out of fifteen, about half, which is the
proportion expected if the two architectures were equivalent and each output had
the same chance of improving or worsening.

The physical quantity is therefore the dimension along which the fifteen outputs
need separate parameters, and this is consistent with three configurations that
differ only in the position of the control rods and share the same materials,
the same geometry and the same fine grid. The metamodel used in the
optimization retains the configuration branches, which are already present
when the ablation is performed and add $3\%$ to the number of parameters, and
the ablation gives no reason to retrain the networks without them.

\section{Workflow}

\subsection{The Scale of the Joint Fitness}

The scaling of Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:scaling}) maps each fitness function onto
$[1,100]$, rescaling it continually between the smallest and the largest value
encountered in the run. The choice makes the comparison internally consistent
within a generation, since the population is ranked against its own extremes,
but it ties the scale to how much that particular run has explored. Within a
run, the scale moves as new extremes appear, so the joint fitness of an
individual is not the same number at generation ten and at generation ninety,
and the formulation compensates by re-evaluating the whole population at every
generation before the survivors are selected. Between runs the scales are
simply different, so the final values are not comparable at all: a search of
500 generations reports a final fitness higher than that of 300 generations on
the same problem setup, for no other reason than having widened its own window.

The search process is left as it stands. The moving scale, with the
re-evaluation of the population at every generation, is the formulation used
in \citet{prevMulti}, and the genetic algorithm optimizes exactly that
objective. A second step is added after the searches have ended. The
comparison of a thousand independent searches requires a single axis, which is
obtained by replacing the two extremes of Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:scaling}) with the
smallest and the largest value that each fitness function has taken across all
the searches, and by re-expressing every outcome on that window. The window is
a property of the campaign as a whole, and since the searches are mutually
independent and already complete when it is built, it plays no role in any of
them. This common scale, and the fitness values expressed through it, are
referred to as ``canonical'' in the following; the ranking of the candidates,
the curve at constrained group count and every comparison reported below are
based on it.

The two ends of the window are not of the same kind. The upper end is an
effective extreme, that is, the worst value that the function takes over the
whole campaign. The lower end is, for 12 out of 15 functions, the threshold
adopted rather than the smallest value observed, because every value that would
fall below the threshold is raised to it, and values of this kind occur in the
searches; only the three power functions, which do not need any control, are
the exception. Therefore, for a controlled function, reaching a scaled value of one does not require a
structure better than every other evaluated, but only one better than the
threshold.

Two checks confirm that the window built in this way is not an arbitrary
choice. The same window computed over almost the whole training database, a
set built before the searches are run and without reference to their
individuals, assigns to each of the fifteen functions a width that differs from
the one built from the searches by no more than $3\%$. Ranking the
distinct structures returned by the searches with one window and with the other
gives the same best structure and the same fifty best; no structure changes
rank by more than sixteen positions, and the typical shift is one position. The range
that the searches explore on their own is therefore the range of the problem.
The whole campaign is then repeated by placing that fixed window inside the
search, so that the algorithm optimizes the canonical fitness directly instead
of its own moving scale. The thousand searches driven in this way tend to
converge to the same best structure and select most often the same six
boundaries, and the selection frequency of each boundary differs by at most 4 percentage points between the two
campaigns; the fixed scale gives a slightly narrower spread of the outcomes,
the median search ending with a joint fitness $41\%$ above that of the best
structure, against $45\%$ with the moving scale, which is
consistent with a stationary objective being marginally easier to optimize
than a moving one, and this optimization runs faster, since the re-evaluation
of the population becomes unnecessary. The formulation of the search is
nevertheless kept as inherited: nothing in the results depends on the choice
between the moving scale and the fixed window, and the comparison with the
results in \citet{prevMulti} is then made at equal objective. %CV: Ma allora è necessario dire tutto quelle cose sulla scala che cambia o che rimane fissa?? 

\subsection{Multiple Independent Searches}

Every search evaluates its individuals with the metamodel only, and the
structure it returns is recomputed with FRENETIC afterwards, as described
further on. The searches use the settings listed in
Table~\tref{eg:tab:gasettings}. The implementation is built on the
\textit{pymoo} library~\cite{pymoo} and reproduces the scheme of
\citet{prevMulti} in its representation, operators and fitness functions. The
population size is established by trial searches: a population of 40
individuals yields a median final joint fitness $1.7$ times higher and a
population of 200 improves it by $17\%$ at twice the cost per search. The number
of generations is checked on the campaign itself: half of the searches have a
joint fitness within $1\%$ of their final value by generation 36, and 85
searches out of a thousand are still improving after generation 90.
The population is kept at 100 and the computing time is spent on the number of
independent searches instead, a choice quantified in the following section.

One implementation detail deserves mention because it affects reproducibility.
The sampling and mutation operators of this problem draw from the global random
generator, which the optimization library leaves unseeded, so that the seed
passed to the optimizer has no effect and successive runs are not reproducible.
Seeding the global generator explicitly before each tribe restores bit-level
reproducibility, and all results presented here can be regenerated exactly.

The searches are mutually independent and are executed in parallel. One thousand
of them require $841$~s on 14 processes of two threads each, corresponding to
about 6.5 core-hours, so that exploring the design space with a thousand
searches costs less than two hundred evaluations with FRENETIC, that is, less
than two generations of a single search run on the full-core model.

\subsection{Verification with FRENETIC}

Every distinct structure produced by the searches is submitted to FRENETIC, and
the ranking reported in the following is the one obtained from the recomputed
values, expressed on the canonical scale. No quantity discussed in the following is a prediction of the metamodel.
This arrangement is deliberate: the metamodel is accurate
enough to rank the candidates and thus to identify a short list worth a
full-core calculation, which is a considerably weaker requirement than
predicting the fitness of the best structures to the precision needed to tell
them apart.

end on the same structure; the distinct structures have between 9 and 20

\subsection{How Many Searches Are Enough}\label{eg:sec:howmany}

The size of the campaign is not chosen \emph{a priori}: whether a thousand
searches are necessary is assessed on the campaign itself, by drawing at random,
without replacement, subsets of $n$ of the thousand outcomes. The question has
three answers depending on what the subsets are required to provide, and
Fig.~\tref{eg:fig:howmany} illustrates them separately.

The first concerns the optimum, and it is the result that motivates this work.
Table~\tref{eg:tab:howmany} reports how close the best of $n$ searches comes to
the best obtained with $n = 1000$. A single search lands $45\%$ above it, and
three searches, the campaign affordable with FRENETIC, land $20\%$ above; ten
searches reach $9\%$, thirty reach $5\%$ and a hundred reach $2\%$, which is
the distance of the second-best structure: the best one is returned by a
single search out of the thousand, so that even a hundred searches typically
stop at the second. Read the other way, the probability that the best of $n$
searches has a joint fitness, as recomputed by FRENETIC, no more than $5\%$
above that of the best structure of the whole campaign is $2\%$ for one
search, $7\%$ for three and $92\%$ for a hundred. The gain from repeating the
search is therefore neither marginal nor unbounded: it is concentrated between
one search and a few tens of them, and a campaign of that size is affordable
with the metamodel but not with FRENETIC in its place.

The second concerns the regularities of the optimized structures; in this
case, the campaign is sufficient. What those regularities rest on is the
frequency with which each of the 29 energy boundaries of the fine grid is
selected, and a hundred searches already fix every one of those frequencies to
within ten percentage points of the value obtained from the thousand, and to

margins, but they cannot add boundaries to the fine grid, which is what would
add anything new to the comparison.

The third aspect is the one that the current set of outcomes leaves open, as
shown by the right panel of Fig.~\tref{eg:fig:howmany}: the number of distinct
structures is still growing at the thousandth search. Of the 747 structures
obtained, 619 are produced by exactly one search. Since $619/747 \simeq 5/6$,
about five structures out of six have been seen only once, which suggests that
the set is incomplete. A further search returns a structure already obtained
in about 4 cases out of 10. A complete census of the reachable structures is

census of the species of the sea from what his net brings up. As long as nearly
every haul brings a species not seen before, each haul adds to what is known,
but it also says that the census is far from complete; the census can be
considered close to exhaustive only when the same, already known, species keep
returning, haul after haul. Here, 6 hauls out of 10 still bring a species
never seen before. % 6 out 10 è giusto? guardando il grafico sembra di più..

\subsection{Recurring Features of the Optimized Structures}\label{eg:sec:recurring}

The 747 structures display regularities which can be examined collectively, and which constitute the general information that
a single search cannot provide.

Table~\tref{eg:tab:boundaries} reports the frequency of each of the 29 internal
boundaries of the fine grid among the optimized structures. The distribution is broad, and no
boundary is common to the solutions: the most frequent, at $0.183$~MeV, appears

$6.70\times10^{7}$, against $9.96\times10^{7}$ for those that adopt
$0.302$~MeV, and those that adopt neither are worse than both.

The placement of the recurring boundaries admits a physical reading which is not
the one that intuition suggests. A natural expectation is that boundaries should
be placed where the spectrum changes shape most abruptly, since a single group
cannot represent a rapidly varying flux, and that they should follow the flux
itself, since a group carrying little flux contributes little to the reaction
rates. The structures say otherwise. The three boundaries of the fast region
where the spectrum bends most sharply, at $1.35$, $0.821$ and $0.498$~MeV, are
used by at most one structure in fifty, although the region they subdivide,
between $2.231$ and $0.302$~MeV, carries $23\%$ of the flux.
Figure~\tref{eg:fig:boundaries} sets the selection frequency against the
importance surrounding the boundary and against the variation of the flux shape
across it, and it shows no clear trend with either quantity over the 29
boundaries. The figure does show, however, that the two readings diverge in the
fast region, for a physical reason. The flux fitness function of
Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:distributed}) measures the flux error of every group weighted
by the total importance $\phi\phi^{\dagger}/v$, so that the relevant measure for
each group is related to the importance it carries. The adjoint flux of this core varies by less
than a factor of two over the whole energy range, so the importance follows
essentially the neutron density $n=\phi/v$: the fast groups carry a substantial part of the
flux but, at those speeds, only a small part of the importance, the ratio
between the two fractions rising from $0.14$ in the highest group to unity
around $10$~keV. The optimizer places its boundaries according to the importance
$n\phi^{\dagger}$ and not according to the flux, and the two regions of the
spectrum show it. Above $0.3$~MeV, the groups on either side of a boundary carry between $0.2$ and
$2.7\%$ of the importance, and the structures keep a single boundary there and
drop the other four; between $0.18$~MeV and $0.75$~keV they carry between $4.4$
and $5.7\%$, an almost flat plateau, and there every boundary is retained by
more than half of the structures.

The tendency can be stated more directly, and in this form it separates the two
readings without ambiguity. In the ten best structures the group that carries
the largest share of the importance carries $11.5\%$ of it, against $17.0\%$ in
structures drawn at random with the same number of groups: the optimized
structures avoid concentrating the importance in one group. Measured on the bare
flux the comparison is reversed, $33.1\%$ against $26.5\%$, so that what the
optimization levels out is the importance and not the flux. The criterion is
never imposed explicitly: it follows from the weighting of the flux term in
Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:distributed}) by the share of the total importance of the
core that each group and assembly carries, Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:weight}), a share
computed on the importance integrated over the volume of each assembly and
normalized to the whole core. This term discriminates among the structures in
the first generations of each search, when three quarters of the population still
differ on it.

campaign, and it is essentially flat over a wide range: six of the group counts
between $G=12$ and $G=20$ lie within $10\%$ of that minimum. Twelve groups are

additional groups used by the best structures bring little further benefit. The

\subsection{Comparison with Reference Structures}

The best verified structure uses $G=15$ groups and reaches a canonical fitness
of $4.4319\times10^{7}$. Its fourteen internal boundaries are marked in the last
column of Table~\tref{eg:tab:boundaries}: it adopts the boundary at $2.231$~MeV,
the alternative that prevails in the fast region, it uses none of the four
boundaries that the searches almost never select, and it does not contain the
boundary at $8.3\times10^{-6}$~MeV that $91\%$ of the structures retain, so
that not even the most frequent boundaries are indispensable. It outperforms all
the $124\,693$ structures computed, by $28\%$ with respect to the best of them.

The comparison that matters most is with the three searches from independent
seeds reported in \citet{prevMulti}, whose structures are the outcome of the
same problem solved with FRENETIC inside the loop and are recomputed here on
the canonical scale from the boundaries reported in that work. In
Fig.~\tref{eg:fig:tradeoff} they lie above the curve of the best fitness found,
by a factor between $1.6$ and $7.3$ at equal group count. Compared with the
faded cloud of the thousand searches, the structure at $G=14$ is a typical
outcome of the campaign at that group count, the one at $G=20$ lies in its
upper tail and the one at $G=13$ is worse than all the structures the campaign
returns at that group count. The gap is therefore a question of how many
searches are affordable, and, with FRENETIC inside the loop, three searches from
independent seeds are what a reasonable budget allows. The objective is the same in the two
cases, so the comparison only measures the size of the campaign.

The third reference is the fine grid itself, whose thirty groups are kept and
no condensation is applied. The collapsing routine only accepts structures with
fewer groups than the fine grid, so this case is computed by running FRENETIC
directly on the 30-group data. No condensation error is therefore present, yet
the result does not come close to the reference: the joint fitness,
$8.049\times10^{13}$, is worse than that of both structures built with
standard spacing rules.

The optimized 15-group structure is therefore better than the fine grid by six
orders of magnitude on the joint fitness. Since that fitness is a product of
fifteen scaled terms, the factor compounds differences which are individually
moderate, and it is instructive to look at the terms one by one in
Table~\tref{eg:tab:standard}. The fine grid is better than the optimized
structure on one of the fifteen quantities, the delayed neutron fraction with
the rods inserted, equal on the three flux terms, where both stand at the lower
end of the window, and worse on the other eleven. Where it loses most is on the
integral parameters: the deviation on the multiplication factor with the rods
partially inserted is $235$~pcm against $9.6$~pcm, and the deviation on the
delayed neutron fraction in the same configuration is $21.6$ against
$2.0$~pcm.

The interpretation is that these residual deviations are not caused by the
energy grid but by the limits of the deterministic model. The fine grid
involves no condensation, so the remaining error, measured against a Monte
Carlo reference, is due to the deterministic model itself, namely the diffusion
approximation and the spatial homogenization. The optimization cannot remove that error, but can compensate
for it: a coarser structure introduces a condensation error of its own, and the
search selects the structure whose condensation error best compensates for the
model error. The fine grid leaves no room for such a compensation.
The fifteen-group structure reproduces the multiplication factor with the rods
partially inserted almost exactly not because it condenses better, but because
its condensation error and the model error nearly compensate each other.

Two consequences follow. The first is practical: refining the energy grid is not
a way of approaching the Monte Carlo reference in this framework, since the
limits of the diffusion model remain; a carefully chosen coarse structure is
worth more than the fine grid itself, and this is the strongest
justification for optimizing the structure at all.

The second consequence concerns the scope of the result. If the quality of a
structure rests on a compensation, the structure is bound to everything that
fixes the two errors being compensated: the core analysed and the operating
states over which the search is conducted; the deterministic scheme, diffusion
with assembly-wise homogenization, whose error it compensates for, an error
that a transport solver or a different homogenization would not share; the reference
and its precision, which enters the objective through the clipping thresholds;
the objective itself, in the quantities chosen and in the way they are combined
by Eq.~(\ref{eg:eqn:joint}); and the boundaries of the 30-group fine grid,
outside which no search can place a cut. This delimits the result without
weakening it: what carries over to another core, solver or set of target
quantities is the procedure, not the fourteen boundaries found here. Contrary to
the intuition that a better structure should also be a more general one, the
closer a structure comes to the reference, the more tightly it is tied to the
particular pair of errors it is tuned to reduce.

\subsection{Accuracy of the Metamodel on the Proposed Structures}\label{eg:sec:accuracy}

metamodel to be judged on exactly the population it proposes and on the quantity the search uses, the joint fitness.

fitness over the 747 structures is
$2.0\%$, while the accuracy on the fifteen quantities themselves is $0.39\%$,
the same as on the external
validation set. The points fall on both sides of the diagonal, $420$ of them
below and $327$ above, so that the metamodel is no more inclined to flatter a
structure than to penalize it. The two orderings agree where the workflow needs them to: the

\section{Conclusions}

The dependence of the optimized energy structure on the random seed used in
genetic algorithms, reported in the literature, is investigated more thoroughly
here by performing one thousand independent searches. The
number is made accessible by a neural metamodel of the fifteen fitness
functions, trained on more than $90\,000$ full-core evaluations and accurate to a
median relative error of $0.403\%$ on structures never seen during training and
of $0.39\%$ on the 747 structures the searches go on to propose, which is the
population on which its accuracy matters. Every one of those structures is
recomputed with FRENETIC, on which the conclusions therefore rest; the agreement
between the metamodel and the solver on the ranking of the structures is
sufficiently good.

A first result of this analysis is the observation of a strong dependence on the
seed. A single search returns a structure whose joint fitness is typically
$45\%$ above the best, and three searches, the campaign affordable with
FRENETIC, reach $20\%$; a hundred searches reach $2\%$
and find a structure within $5\%$ of the best in $92\%$ of the cases. The gain
from repeating the search is concentrated in a range of campaign sizes that
cannot be afforded with FRENETIC inside the loop, which is the argument for
replacing it there.

The landscape that the thousand searches map is rugged. They converge to 747
distinct structures, $83\%$ of which are produced by a single search, and two
structures taken at random disagree on $33\%$ of the twenty-nine available
boundaries. Only two boundaries are retained by more than $90\%$ of the
structures, and the best structure lacks one of them, so that the solutions
share no common skeleton. What they do share is an exclusive choice in the
fast region: no structure retains both the boundary at $2.231$ and the one at
$0.302$~MeV, and the first, adopted by $89\%$ of the solutions, is also the
better one, by a third in median fitness.

Where the boundaries go is not where the spectrum bends most nor where the flux
is: the three boundaries of the fast region where the spectrum changes most
abruptly are almost never selected, although the region they subdivide carries
a quarter of the flux, and what decides is the neutron importance with which the
objective function weights the flux error. The optimized
structures distribute that importance among their groups $1.5$ times more evenly
than random structures with the same number of groups, while on the bare flux
they are less even than random ones: the optimization moves towards an
equipartition of the importance, a criterion never imposed explicitly which
follows from the weighting of the flux fitness function.

The best structure identified employs fifteen groups. It improves by $28\%$ on
the best of the $124\,693$ structures computed, on structures built by uniform spacing rules by three to five orders
of magnitude, and on the three structures obtained with the solver inside the
loop by factors between $1.7$ and $8.1$. The last comparison is the one that
measures the original contribution of this work, and the gap is a question of how many searches
are affordable rather than of how the objective is posed.

The workflow itself is transferable. Nothing in it is specific to this core
beyond the training set: the metamodel replaces the solver only inside the
search, the solver retains the last word, and the cost of building the metamodel
is repaid after nine searches and then amortized over every subsequent analysis
performed on the same reactor. The analyses presented here would have required
of the order of $390\,000$ core-hours of FRENETIC and are carried
out for $29$.

Two directions are left open. The accuracy of the metamodel is still limited by
the amount of training data, as the learning curve shows, so that a further
campaign of FRENETIC calculations would still improve it. The description of the structure
given to the network could also be enriched with physics-informed quantities,
such as the lethargy widths of the condensed groups or the variation of the
reference spectrum within them weighted by the importance; a preliminary attempt
in this direction does not lower the error, but the possibilities are far from
exhausted and the question deserves a study of its own.
