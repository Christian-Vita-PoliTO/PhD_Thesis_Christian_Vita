\chapter{Reflector and Core Temperature Corrections}\label{ch:correction-ratios}

\section{Scope}

The database of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} is built on isothermal states: in
every entry the fuel, the moderator, the reflector and the structures are at
one common temperature. Two effects of an operating core therefore lie outside
what the interpolation of Eq.~(\ref{cs:eq:interp}) can represent, and both are
temperature effects that the coefficients cannot see.

The first is the temperature difference between the core and the reflector.
The coefficients of the fission matrix are defined between fissile cells, so
the temperature that enters the interpolation is that of the fuel; the
reflector enters only through the isothermal entries of the database, and a
core hotter than its reflector has no entry to draw on. The second is a sharp
temperature gradient inside the core. The interpolation assigns to each
coefficient the temperature of its destination cell alone, so a cell that is
much hotter than its neighbours is treated as if the whole core were at its
own temperature.

Both are treated with the scheme of Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:ratios}: a ratio of two
matrices tallied at reference conditions that differ in the effect of interest
alone, applied element by element to the interpolated matrix and scaled by a
weight that measures how much of the reference effect is present at the target
state. The first correction, the Reflector Correction Ratio, is developed on
the three-dimensional VTB model of Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:model}, the same used for
the criticality search. The second, the Core Temperature Correction Ratio, is
developed on a two-dimensional variant of the same design, described where it
is introduced. This chapter also contains the examination of the statistical
uncertainty of the interpolated results that Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:uq} announced:
the correlations between the tallied coefficients, their analytical modelling,
and the validation of the resulting estimate against independent replicas of
the whole database.

\section{The Reflector Correction Ratio}\label{cr:sec:rcr}

\subsection{The reflector in the neutron balance}

Reflectors reduce the leakage of a core by scattering escaping neutrons back
into it. In a micro reactor the effect is particularly strong, because the core
is compact and the fissile inventory limited, so that the reflector shapes the
flux and not only its magnitude. In the VTB design the reflector also houses
the control drums, so its state changes during operation in two ways at once,
through its temperature and through the rotation of the absorbers.

The mechanism by which the reflector enters a coefficient is the following. A
neutron born in a peripheral cell leaks out of the active region, interacts
with the reflector, and a fraction of the neutrons is scattered back into the
core, where further fissions are induced. The process involves both cells of a
generic coefficient $a_{ij}$, the one where the neutrons are born and the one
where the fissions are induced, so a correction built as a ratio of two
complete fission matrices retains the dependence on both indices.
Figure~\tref{cr:fig:gradient} shows a temperature field of the kind the
correction is meant for, with the fuel and the reflector at different uniform
temperatures.

\subsection{Definition}\label{cr:sec:rcr-def}

The corrected matrix enters the eigenvalue problem in place of the interpolated
one,
%
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \frac{1}{\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}}}\sum_{j} \tilde a^{*}_{ij}\,F_j
      = \frac{1}{\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}}}\sum_{j} r_{ij}\,\tilde a_{ij}\,F_j,
  \label{cr:eq:corrected-eigen}
\end{equation}
%
and the Reflector Correction Ratio $r_{ij}$ is defined as
%
\begin{equation}
  r_{ij}(\Delta T_i,\Delta T_j) \;=\;
  \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\Delta T_i + \Delta T_j}{T_{\max,\mathrm{DB}} - T_{\min,\mathrm{DB}}}
  \left( \frac{a^{\Delta T}_{ij}}{\tilde a_{ij}(\tilde T_{f,i})} - 1 \right) + 1 ,
  \label{cr:eq:rcr1}
\end{equation}
%
where $\Delta T_k = T_{f,k} - T_{r,k}$ is the local temperature difference of
the fissile cell $k$, $T_{r,k}$ being the temperature of the closest
non-fissile region, and $a^{\Delta T}_{ij}$ is a matrix tallied once with the
fuel at $T_{\max,\mathrm{DB}}=\SI{1000}{K}$ and the reflector at
$T_{\min,\mathrm{DB}}=\SI{300}{K}$. The denominator is the interpolated
coefficient at the temperature of the destination cell, so that the ratio
compares two matrices that differ in the core--reflector difference alone.

Two assumptions are made. The first is that the departure of each coefficient
from its uniform-temperature value scales linearly with the local temperature
difference between fuel and reflector; under this assumption a single
reference case serves every gradient, instead of one database entry per
gradient. The
second concerns the localization of the weight. A coefficient describes a
transfer between two cells, each with its own thermal environment, so the
difference entering the weight is taken as the average of the differences of
the two cells. Formulations in which the weight depends on a single index were
also tested: they coincide with Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:rcr1}) whenever the local
difference is spatially uniform, differ only in the non-uniform cases, and give
larger deviations on the multiplication factor there. The same construction
appeared, in single-index form, in the correction of \citet{HeANE2019}, where
the factor is a ratio of fission rates tallied at a given position under an
imposed uniform source and is therefore a single-index quantity by
construction, whereas the ratio adopted here is a ratio of two complete
matrices.

The limiting cases follow from the definition. When fuel and reflector are at
the same temperature in both cells, the weight vanishes, $r_{ij}=1$, and the
interpolated matrix is returned unchanged, which is correct because that is
exactly the condition under which the database was built. When the difference
in both cells equals the one of the reference case,
$T_{f,i}=T_{f,j}=\SI{1000}{K}$ and $T_{r,i}=T_{r,j}=\SI{300}{K}$, the weight is
unity and
%
\begin{equation}
  \tilde a^{*}_{ij} = r_{ij}\,\tilde a_{ij} = a^{\Delta T}_{ij},
  \label{cr:eq:rcr-limit}
\end{equation}
%
so that the matrix tallied with the reference gradient is recovered exactly.
Between the two limits the correction varies linearly with the mean of the two
local differences.

\subsection{Dependence on the drum angle}

Equation~(\ref{cr:eq:rcr1}) accounts for temperature alone. The control drums,
however, sit inside the reflector, and rotating them changes the absorption in
the very region whose effect the ratio describes, most of all at the interface
with the outermost ring of assemblies. Each drum position therefore requires
its own reference matrix, and the ratio becomes
%
\begin{equation}
  r_{ij}(\Delta T_i,\Delta T_j,\theta) \;=\;
  \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\Delta T_i + \Delta T_j}{T_{\max,\mathrm{DB}} - T_{\min,\mathrm{DB}}}
  \left( \frac{\tilde a^{\Delta T}_{ij}(\theta)}{\tilde a_{ij}(\tilde T_{f,i},\tilde\theta)} - 1 \right) + 1 ,
  \label{cr:eq:rcr2}
\end{equation}
%
where the numerator is now interpolated in the drum angle as well. The limiting
cases of Sec.~\ref{cr:sec:rcr-def} are preserved, since at a given drum
position $\tilde a^{\Delta T}_{ij}(\theta)$ plays the role that
$a^{\Delta T}_{ij}$ played before. At $\theta=\ang{0}$ the two formulations
coincide.

The cost of the second formulation is one reference calculation per drum
angle. The whole campaign, database and reference matrices together,
therefore requires
%
\begin{equation}
  N_T N_\theta + N_\theta
  \label{cr:eq:rcr-cost}
\end{equation}
%
criticality calculations, that is, seventeen runs on top of the hundred and
thirty-six of the database, as reported in Table~\tref{cs:tab:cost}.

\subsection{Test cases with a non-uniform core temperature}\label{cr:sec:rcr-nonuniform}

Three further cases replace the uniform fuel temperature with a spatial
distribution, Fig.~\tref{cr:fig:cases567}. In Case 5 the three rings of
assemblies are held at 1000, 750 and \SI{500}{K} from the centre outwards, with
the reflector at \SI{300}{K}: a symmetric but strongly non-uniform profile. In
Case 6 the fuel temperature increases linearly along one direction, from 400 to
\SI{1000}{K}, with the reflector again at \SI{300}{K}: a non-symmetric profile.
In Case 7 the reflector is no longer isothermal but carries a profile of its
own, consistent with the adjacent fuel temperatures, and the fuel follows the
power-like distribution of Eq.~(\ref{cs:eq:correlation}) at nominal power,
between 821 and \SI{879}{K} over the ninety cells. The local difference between
fuel and reflector is then small and changes sign across the core.

Table~\tref{cr:tab:res567} reports the results, and
Figs.~\tref{cr:fig:case5}--\tref{cr:fig:case7} the cell-wise picture. The
behaviour of the correction is not the same in the three cases, and the
difference is instructive. In Case 6, where the fuel temperature spans
\SI{600}{K} along one direction while the reflector stays at \SI{300}{K}, the
correction removes almost the entire discrepancy on the multiplication factor,
from $-201$ to $+9$~pcm, and halves the root-mean-square deviation of the

profile is steep, the innermost ring being the hottest and the outermost the
coldest, so the local difference between fuel and reflector varies sharply from
ring to ring, while the correction is calibrated on a spatially uniform
gradient. A zonation of the correction, with a reference matrix generated
separately for different regions of the core, is the natural way to address
this and is left to future developments. In Case 7 there is little for the
correction to recover, since the local difference is small and changes sign,
and indeed the corrected and uncorrected results differ by \SI{14}{pcm}, of the
order of the combined uncertainty.

\subsection{Significance of the residual deviations}\label{cr:sec:rcr-budget}

statistical noise of the comparison, computed with the ensemble method of
Sec.~\ref{cs:sec:ensemble}: the database is resampled coefficient by

\section{The Core Temperature Correction Ratio}\label{cr:sec:ctcr}

\subsection{A two-dimensional variant of the model}\label{cr:sec:model2d}

The second correction addresses a different failure of the same interpolation,
and it is developed on a variant of the reactor model, chosen so that the
effect under study is isolated from everything else.

The system is a modified version of the same heat-pipe micro reactor
\cite{Stauff2024}: 127 hexagonal TRISO-fuelled assemblies in a graphite
monolith, surrounded by a beryllium radial reflector, Fig.~\tref{cr:fig:geom2d}.
Two simplifications distinguish it from the model of Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:model}.
The control elements are removed, so that no absorber competes with the
temperature field in shaping the flux, and the core is treated as axially
infinite, so that the analysis is two-dimensional and radial throughout. One
cell is one assembly, and the matrix is $127\times127$, tallied as before from
a single whole-core criticality calculation. The finer radial mesh is what the
study needs: the effect examined here is the influence of the temperature of
the neighbouring cells on a coefficient, and resolving it requires cells that
are individually addressable in temperature, which ninety three-dimensional
zones of thirty assemblies would not provide.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter8/Figs/VTB_core_and_assembly.png}
  \caption[The two-dimensional model]{The two-dimensional variant of the
  reactor model used for the core temperature correction: 127 hexagonal
  assemblies in a graphite monolith surrounded by a beryllium reflector, with
  the detail of one assembly.}
  \label{cr:fig:geom2d}
\end{figure}

The database is the one of Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:database}, eight uniform
temperatures from 300 to \SI{1000}{K} in steps of \SI{100}{K}. With no drums
there is no second axis, so the interpolation of Eq.~(\ref{cs:eq:interp})
reduces to its one-dimensional form,
%
\begin{equation}
  \tilde a_{ij}(\tilde T_i) \;=\; a^{-}_{ij}
  + \frac{\tilde T_i - T^{-}}{T^{+} - T^{-}}\left(a^{+}_{ij} - a^{-}_{ij}\right),
  \label{cr:eq:interp1d}
\end{equation}
%
where $a^{-}_{ij}$ and $a^{+}_{ij}$ are the coefficients at the database
temperatures immediately below and above the temperature of the destination
cell $i$.

\subsection{What the interpolation misses}

Equation~(\ref{cr:eq:interp1d}) treats each coefficient as a function of the
temperature of its destination cell alone, and therefore ignores the state of
the cells around it. The approximation is harmless when the field is smooth,
because neighbouring cells are then at nearly the same temperature as the
destination, and the database entry at that temperature describes the
neighbourhood correctly. It fails when the field is sharp: a cell much hotter
than its surroundings is represented by an entry in which the whole core is as
hot as that cell, and the neutron economy of that entry is not the one of the
real configuration.

The Core Temperature Correction Ratio informs the coefficient about the pair of
temperatures $(T_k,T_l)$ of the two cells it connects, and it is calibrated on
the sharpest field the database can express. Two additional matrices are
tallied, Fig.~\tref{cr:fig:dirac}: one with the central assembly $C$ at the
hottest database temperature, \SI{1000}{K}, and the rest of the core at the
coldest, \SI{300}{K}, and one with the assignment reversed. Both are needed,
because the correction has to handle a hot cell in a colder surrounding and the
opposite case.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter8/Figs/fig_temp_dist_dirac1000.png}
  \caption[Calibration cases of the core temperature correction]{The two
  calibration cases of the core temperature correction: the central assembly at
  \SI{1000}{K} in a core at \SI{300}{K}, and the reverse.}
  \label{cr:fig:dirac}
\end{figure}

Comparing each calibration matrix with what the ordinary interpolation would
give for the same field yields two ratios, functions of the geometric distance
$d$ between the central cell and a generic cell $j$, the case $j=C$ included,
%
\begin{equation}
  r^{+}(d) = \frac{a^{\mathrm{cal},+}_{Cj}}{\tilde a_{Cj}},
  \qquad
  r^{-}(d) = \frac{a^{\mathrm{cal},-}_{Cj}}{\tilde a_{Cj}},
  \label{cr:eq:ctcr-cal}
\end{equation}
%
where the superscript marks the hot-centre and the cold-centre case. The
hexagonal symmetry of the core makes the distance, and not the direction, the
only variable the ratios depend on. Each ratio measures how much the
local-temperature assumption misses in the most extreme configuration the
database can represent; the two together cover a temperature difference of
either sign.

\subsection{Definition}

For an arbitrary temperature field the correction applied to the interpolated
coefficient $\tilde a_{kl}$ is
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat r_{kl}(\Delta T_{kl},d) \;=\;
  \frac{\Delta T_{kl}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{cal}}}\big(r^{\pm}(d) - 1\big) + 1,
  \qquad
  \Delta T_{kl} =
  \begin{cases}
    T_k - T_l, & k \neq l, \\[6pt]
    T_k - \dfrac{1}{|\mathcal N(k)|}\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal N(k)} T_n, & k = l,
  \end{cases}
  \label{cr:eq:ctcr}
\end{equation}
%
where $\Delta T_{\mathrm{cal}} = \SI{700}{K}$ is the temperature span of the
two calibration cases, and $r^{+}$ is used when $\Delta T_{kl}>0$ and $r^{-}$
when $\Delta T_{kl}<0$, each evaluated at the geometric distance between the
two cells. The correction transposes the calibration ratios to any pair of
cells and scales them with the actual temperature difference of that pair,
measured against the difference of the calibration.

The diagonal deserves a comment, because there source and destination coincide
and the difference between two cells is not defined. What the calibration
ratios carry at $d=0$ is how much the interpolation misses the coefficient
$a_{kk}$ of a cell surrounded by a neighbourhood at a different temperature, so
the weight of the diagonal is built with the difference between the cell and
the average of its first ring of neighbours $\mathcal N(k)$. No further
calibration is needed for this case, since $r^{\pm}(0)$ comes from the same two
matrices. The two variants compared in the following are named \emph{raw},
interpolation alone, and \emph{corrected}, interpolation followed by
Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:ctcr}).

\section{Statistical uncertainty of the interpolated matrix}\label{cr:sec:uq}

Section~\ref{fm:sec:uq} left two questions open: how the coefficients tallied
in one criticality calculation are correlated with one another, and how far the
uncorrelated estimate used elsewhere in this Part departs from the truth. Both
are answered here, on the model and the database of Sec.~\ref{cr:sec:model2d}.

\subsection{Four ways of fixing the correlation matrix}\label{cr:sec:four}

The estimate of $\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$ follows the ensemble method of
Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:uq}: the database is perturbed, the interpolation and the
correction are reapplied, the eigenvalue problem is solved, and the spread of
the results over many realizations is the database contribution. Writing the
perturbation of the retained coefficients as
$\boldsymbol\delta\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$, the covariance
factors as
%
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\rho\,\mathbf D,
  \label{cr:eq:sigma-factor}
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathbf D$ the diagonal matrix of the per-coefficient standard deviations
that Serpent reports and $\boldsymbol\rho$ the correlation matrix. The
amplitude $\mathbf D$ is read from the database and is common to every
approach; what has to be decided is $\boldsymbol\rho$. Four ways of deciding it
are compared.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Uncorrelated resampling}, $\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf I$. Each
  coefficient is perturbed on its own with its own variance. This is the
  assumption of earlier work on the uncertainty of fission-matrix results
  \cite{HeWaltersNSE2022} and the natural baseline.
  \item \emph{Analytical model},
  $\boldsymbol\rho=\boldsymbol\rho_{\mathrm{pred}}$, predicted in closed form
  from the branching structure of the fission process, derived in
  Sec.~\ref{cr:sec:derivation}. It requires no Monte Carlo replica beyond the
  database itself.
  \item \emph{Measured correlation}, $\boldsymbol\rho=\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}$,
  obtained from the cycle-by-cycle tallies of one calculation, each active
  cycle treated as a pseudo-replica. Serpent does not write the fission matrix
  per cycle, so this required modifying its source. The matrix is measured once
  at \SI{300}{K} and reused at every database temperature, an assumption tested
  in Sec.~\ref{cr:sec:invariance}.
  \item \emph{Brute force}. The whole database is rebuilt from scratch $150$
  times, with one genuinely independent calculation per temperature and per
  repetition. No model of the covariance is involved: the correlations are
  present in the replicas by construction. It is the most expensive estimate
  and serves as the reference for the other three.
\end{enumerate}

The perturbation is drawn column by column, because the structure derived below
couples only coefficients that share a source column, so $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is
block diagonal with one block per source cell. Factorizing each block as
$\boldsymbol\rho_j = \mathbf C_j\mathbf C_j^{\top}$ by Cholesky decomposition,
the realization of the perturbation of column $j$ at the database temperature
$T$ is
%
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\delta_{:,j}(T) = \mathbf D_j(T)\,\mathbf C_j(T)\,\mathbf z,
  \qquad \mathbf z\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I_N),
  \label{cr:eq:cholesky}
\end{equation}
%
so that the covariance is reproduced by construction. One vector $\mathbf z$ is
shared by the coefficients of the same column, and this sharing is what
imposes the correlation, while the marginal standard deviation of each coefficient stays
the one Serpent reports whatever $\boldsymbol\rho$ is. The eight database
temperatures are independent calculations, so their perturbations are drawn
independently. The ensemble has $M=500$ realizations throughout this section.

\subsection{The correlation of the coefficients of one column}\label{cr:sec:derivation}

Coefficients tallied within one criticality calculation are not independent,
because the number of histories simulated per active cycle is fixed, a fact
behind well documented biases and correlations of cycle-based Monte Carlo
estimators \cite{BrissendenGarlick1986}. For two coefficients that share a
source column the mechanism can be made explicit.

At the start of a cycle the number $N_j$ of source neutrons born in cell $j$ is
fixed, inherited from the fission bank of the previous cycle. Each of them,
independently of the others, either causes a fission in a destination cell $i$,
with probability $\pi_i$, or elsewhere. The tracking used here is analog, with
no variance reduction, and a history is terminated when it causes a fission, so
a history contributes to one destination only and all its $\bar\nu$ secondaries
are born there. Writing the contribution of history $s$ to the destination $i$
as $X^{(i)}_s = \bar\nu\,I_s(i)$, where $I_s(i)$ is one if the history fissions
in $i$ and zero otherwise, the coefficient is the average per source neutron,
%
\begin{equation}
  a_{ij} = \frac{1}{N_j}\sum_{s=1}^{N_j} X^{(i)}_s .
  \label{cr:eq:aij}
\end{equation}
%
Since $I_s(i)\,I_s(l) = 0$ for $i\neq l$ and different histories are
independent, the standard multinomial results \cite{CasellaBerger2002} give,
for one history and then for the coefficient,
%
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(a_{ij}) = \frac{\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i(1-\pi_i)}{N_j},
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{lj}) = -\frac{\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i\pi_l}{N_j},
  \qquad i\neq l .
  \label{cr:eq:multinomial}
\end{equation}
%
The covariance is negative for every pair of destinations that share a source
column, and the reason is exhaustion: a fixed number of histories is split,
exhaustively and exclusively, among the destinations, so more of them reaching
$i$ means fewer reaching $l$. Dividing the covariance by the geometric mean of
the two variances, the factor $\bar\nu^2/N_j$ cancels, and what survives
depends only on the destination probabilities, not on the number of histories,
which is a run-time setting and not a property of the physics,
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,l) = -\sqrt{\frac{\pi_i}{1-\pi_i}\cdot\frac{\pi_l}{1-\pi_l}} .
  \label{cr:eq:rho-pi}
\end{equation}
%
The probabilities are not tallied directly, but the mean coefficients are, and
$a_i = \bar\nu\pi_i$. Writing $\kappa=-1/\bar\nu$ and substituting,
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,l) = \kappa\,
  \frac{\sqrt{a_i\,a_l}}{\sqrt{(1+\kappa a_i)(1+\kappa a_l)}},
  \label{cr:eq:rho-pred}
\end{equation}
%
a closed form that needs only the multiplicity and the mean coefficients of the
database itself. Figure~\tref{cr:fig:corr} compares the predicted matrix with
the one measured from the cycle-by-cycle tallies at \SI{300}{K}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter8/Figs/correlation_matrices_300K.png}
  \caption[Correlation of the tallied coefficients]{Correlation matrix of the
  coefficients of one source column at \SI{300}{K}: predicted in closed form by
  Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:rho-pred}) and measured from the cycle-by-cycle tallies.}
  \label{cr:fig:corr}
\end{figure}

Two simplifications enter the derivation. The multiplicity is treated as a
constant, whereas it depends on the incident energy and every cell sees a
slightly different spectrum, and every history is given unit weight, whereas
Monte Carlo weights are not exactly one. Neither touches the amplitude of the
perturbation, which is read from the statistical errors Serpent reports and
therefore already contains both effects, and neither touches the covariance of
Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:multinomial}), which vanishes term by term as long as a history
fissions at most once. Both change only the variance that normalizes it.
Writing $c_i^2$ for the relative variance of the score of one event, the
correlation becomes
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,l) = -\sqrt{
  \frac{\pi_i}{1-\pi_i+c_i^2}\cdot\frac{\pi_l}{1-\pi_l+c_l^2}} ,
  \label{cr:eq:rho-c2}
\end{equation}
%
and Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:rho-pred}) is the case $c^2=0$. For these calculations
$c^2$ is small: the energy dependence of the multiplicity contributes
$1.5\times10^{-4}$, obtained from the eleven-group multiplicity, fission cross
section and flux of the reference calculation and corresponding to a
\SI{1.24}{\percent} spread of $\bar\nu$ across the fission spectrum, and the
weights contribute a comparable amount, since they depart from unity only
through the implicit treatment of $(n,xn)$ reactions, which accounts for
$1.2\times10^{-4}$ of the source weight. Propagating $c^2=1.5\times10^{-4}$
through the whole chain changes $\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$ by
\SI{0.004}{\percent} on the multiplication factor and by \SI{0.002}{\percent}
on the root-mean-square of the source; assigning the whole \SI{1.24}{\percent}
spread as an error on $\bar\nu$ changes them by \SI{0.33}{\percent} and
\SI{0.14}{\percent}. For reference, the core-average multiplicity moves by
\SI{0.018}{\percent} over the whole database range.

\subsection{Coefficients of different columns}

The derivation says nothing about two coefficients that do not share a column.
It would be convenient to argue that the numbers of source neutrons of two
different cells are independent, but they are not: the total population per
cycle is fixed, so the source cells compete for a shared budget. That coupling,
however, need not reach the normalized coefficients. By the law of total
covariance,
%
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{kj'}) =
  \underbrace{E\big[\operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{kj'}\mid N_j,N_{j'})\big]}_{\text{(I)}}
  + \underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}\big(E[a_{ij}\mid N_j],E[a_{kj'}\mid N_{j'}]\big)}_{\text{(II)}} .
  \label{cr:eq:total-cov}
\end{equation}
%
Term (I) vanishes because histories born in disjoint cells are conditionally
independent once the two populations are given. Term (II) vanishes because
$E[a_{ij}\mid N_j] = \pi_i$ does not depend on the realized population, by
construction of the normalized estimator of Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:aij}).

This rules out one channel, the competition for a shared budget, not every
conceivable one. A bootstrap check on the $150$ independent replicas at
\SI{300}{K} does find a small residual: pairs of coefficients that share a
column are negatively correlated \SI{59.95}{\percent} of the time, with a
\SI{95}{\percent} confidence interval of 59.48 to \SI{60.30}{\percent}, against
\SI{51.13}{\percent} for pairs that share a destination row and
\SI{51.20}{\percent} for pairs that share neither. The two latter figures are
distinguishable from the \SI{50}{\percent} of no correlation, but by less than
a tenth of the same-column effect. The analytical model neglects that residual,
and the consequence of doing so is measured in Sec.~\ref{cr:sec:validation}
against the brute-force reconstruction, which makes no such assumption.

\subsection{Temperature invariance of the measured matrix}\label{cr:sec:invariance}

The third method measures the correlation at \SI{300}{K} and reuses it at every
other temperature. The simplest test of that assumption is to measure the
matrix independently at the opposite end of the range and ask whether the two
differ by more than the noise of estimating either one. Differences are
measured with the relative Frobenius distance,
%
\begin{equation}
  d_F(\mathbf A,\mathbf B) = \frac{\|\mathbf A-\mathbf B\|_F}{\|\mathbf B\|_F},
  \qquad
  \|\mathbf M\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{a}\sum_{b} M_{ab}^2},
  \label{cr:eq:frobenius}
\end{equation}
%
which is zero for identical matrices and of order one for matrices that differ
by as much as their own magnitude. The distance between the matrices at
\SI{300}{K} and at \SI{1000}{K} is $0.643$. The noise floor, obtained by
splitting the cycle data of one temperature in two halves and measuring the
matrix from each, is $0.909$ at both temperatures. The distance between the two
extremes of the database is therefore smaller than the fluctuation of a single
estimate under its own finite statistics, so reusing one matrix across the
database is at least as defensible as trusting the estimate at any one
temperature.

\subsection{Validation against independent replicas}\label{cr:sec:validation}

The reference calculations carry, averaged over the twelve cases, an
uncertainty of \SI{9.7}{pcm} on the multiplication factor and
\SI{0.309}{\percent} on the root-mean-square of the source,
Fig.~\tref{cr:fig:sigref}. The one mild outlier is the case with the broadest
temperature profile, 12.5~pcm and \SI{0.42}{\percent}, whose flatter fission
source makes the detector tally noisier. That is half of the budget; the other
half is the database term, and Fig.~\tref{cr:fig:validation} compares the three
inexpensive estimates of it with the brute-force reconstruction, case by case
and for both observables.

The ordering of the three is as expected in almost every case. The uncorrelated
baseline gives systematically the largest uncertainty, and therefore the widest
band, the only exception being the multiplication factor of one case, which is
within the fluctuation of a resampling procedure; the analytical model gives
the smallest values, and the measured correlation lies in between. On the
multiplication factor the analytical model reproduces the brute-force
uncertainty to within \SI{4.6}{\percent}, ahead of the measured correlation,
\SI{8.7}{\percent}, and well ahead of the uncorrelated baseline,
\SI{18.0}{\percent}. The reason is the sign of the intra-column correlations:
they are negative, so neglecting them inflates the propagated variance of the
eigenvalue. On the root-mean-square of the source the ranking changes, the
measured correlation reaching \SI{3.3}{\percent} and the analytical model
\SI{3.6}{\percent}, against \SI{7.3}{\percent} of the uncorrelated baseline.
This too is consistent: the source is a less global quantity than the
eigenvalue and is sensitive to the correlations between different columns,
which are precisely what the analytical model leaves out.

One objection to the analytical model is that it is not free of Monte Carlo
noise after all, since Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:rho-pred}) is evaluated on the mean
coefficients of one database, themselves uncertain. Rebuilding the predicted
matrix from each of the $150$ independent replicas at \SI{300}{K} and comparing
it with the one built from the production database gives a mean relative
Frobenius distance of $0.0013$ and a maximum of $0.0014$, two to three orders
of magnitude below both the temperature dependence of the measured matrix and
its own noise floor. The predicted matrix can therefore be treated as
deterministic.

The analytical model is adopted in the rest of this chapter. The choice rests
on its accuracy, on the fact that it needs no calculation beyond the database,
and on the acceptance test it produces: giving the smallest uncertainty, it
yields the narrowest window within which a residual can be declared compatible
with noise, and is therefore the strictest of the four.

database noise has itself been validated against brute force, the most likely
cause is the finite spacing of the temperature grid, whose eight points cannot
resolve the sharpest gradients.

\section{The two corrections together}\label{cr:sec:combined}

The two corrections act on the same interpolated matrix and share the structure
of Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:ratios}, but they are calibrated on different reference
cases and answer different questions. Their relation is stated here precisely.

The reflector correction is a function of the difference between a fissile cell
and the non-fissile region closest to it, and its reference case is a core hot
and a reflector cold. It acts most strongly on the cells that face the
reflector, and it is indispensable when the core is uniformly hot: without it
the deviation of the multiplication factor reaches several hundred pcm,
Table~\tref{cr:tab:res14}. The core temperature correction is a function of the
difference between two fissile cells, and its reference case is one assembly
hot in a cold core. It acts on the cells that surround a sharp internal
feature, and it is indispensable when the field inside the core is spatially
sharp, Table~\tref{cr:tab:zones}.

The two therefore do not overlap in what they correct, and the results of this
chapter show each one leaving the other's effect untouched. The residual offset
of the replica clouds of Fig.~\tref{cr:fig:keffdist} is the reflector effect
seen from within the core temperature study, since the test fields of that
study hold the reflector at \SI{300}{K} while the database is uniform; and
Case 5 of Sec.~\ref{cr:sec:rcr-nonuniform}, where the reflector correction
recovers less than elsewhere, has a ring-wise field whose internal steps are
exactly what the core temperature correction is calibrated on.

Applying both at once is the natural next step, and it is not a matter of
multiplying the two ratios and stopping there. They are calibrated on two
different models, the three-dimensional core with drums and the
two-dimensional core without them, so a combined application requires the
calibration of both on the same model. Their weights would also have to be
made compatible, since one is built on the difference between a cell and the
reflector and the other on the difference between two cells, and a field that
is both hot overall and internally sharp would otherwise be counted twice in
the region where the two overlap, the outermost ring of assemblies. The
combined correction is left to future work, and the results of this chapter
delimit what it would have to reconcile.

\section{Summary}

Two effects that the isothermal database cannot represent have been corrected
with the scheme of Sec.~\ref{fm:sec:ratios}, each with its own reference case
and its own weight.

The reflector correction restores the temperature difference between the core
and the reflector. On a uniformly hot core it reduces the deviation of the
multiplication factor from several hundred pcm to a few tens, and below the
statistical uncertainty of the reference calculations once the reference matrix
follows the rotation of the drums, at a cost of one additional calculation per
drum angle. On non-uniform fields it recovers almost the whole discrepancy when
the field is smooth, and only part of it when the profile is steep from ring to
ring, a limit that a zonation of the reference matrix would address.

The core temperature correction restores the influence of the neighbouring
cells on a coefficient, and is calibrated on the sharpest field the database
can express. Over twelve fields it reduces the mean absolute deviation of the
multiplication factor by \SI{42}{\percent} and that of the fission source by
\SI{26}{\percent}, with a benefit concentrated, by construction, on the sharp
and localized profiles: for the ring-shaped fields the number of cells whose
local deviation is consistent with the noise roughly doubles.

The statistical uncertainty of the interpolated results has been examined in
full. The coefficients that share a source column are negatively correlated,
for a reason that is exhaustion of a fixed number of histories among mutually
exclusive destinations, and that correlation is predicted in closed form from
the mean coefficients and the multiplicity alone. Against a brute-force
reconstruction from $150$ independent databases, the closed form reproduces the
uncertainty of the multiplication factor to within \SI{4.6}{\percent}, the
measured correlation to \SI{8.7}{\percent} and the uncorrelated assumption used
elsewhere in this Part to \SI{18.0}{\percent}, the last one always in excess,
so that the bands reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} are conservative.
On the fission source the ordering changes, and the residual correlation
between different columns, which the closed form omits, is what accounts for
the difference.
