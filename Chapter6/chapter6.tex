\chapter{The Fission Matrix Method for Heat-Pipe Micro Reactors}\label{ch:fm-theory}

\section{Why a reduced-order model}

Heat-pipe micro reactors (HPMRs) are controlled by drums rotating in the radial
reflector. Their design and their operation ask for the same calculation over
and over: at a given power, at a given point of the cycle, which drum angle
makes the core critical, and what does the core look like there. A full-core
Monte Carlo calculation answers the question for one state at a cost of tens of
CPU hours. It is affordable for a handful of states and not for the thousands
that a design space, a fuel cycle or an online monitoring tool require.

The fission matrix (FM) method \cite{CarneyANE2014} is a way out. A single
Monte Carlo calculation is made to tally, besides its usual results, a matrix
that couples every fissile region of the core to every other. That matrix
carries the eigenvalue and the fission source of the state it was tallied at,
and it can be stored. A database of matrices tallied at a set of reference
states then represents any intermediate state by interpolation, at the cost of
an eigenproblem of modest size: seconds instead of hours, with no Monte Carlo
run in the loop.

The three works of this Part build on that idea for the same reactor and with
the same tools. Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} builds the database over the fuel
temperature and the drum angle, uses it for a criticality search, and extends it
along the cycle with a correction for burnup. Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios}
deals with what the interpolation cannot see, the temperature of the reflector
and sharp temperature gradients inside the core, through two multiplicative
corrections. This chapter gathers what the two share: the definition of the
matrix and the way it is generated, the reactor model, the interpolation
scheme, the common form of the corrections, the treatment of the statistical
uncertainty and the metrics used to judge the results.

\section{The fission matrix}\label{fm:sec:definition}

The fissile domain is divided into $N$ regions, and the neutron transport
between them is described over one averaged generation. Integrating the
$k$-eigenvalue transport equation over the volume of a source region $j$ and
of a destination region $i$ yields an algebraic eigenvalue problem
\cite{CarneyANE2014},
%
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{eff}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N} a_{ij}\,F_j
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{F} = k_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathbf{F},
  \label{fm:eq:eigen}
\end{equation}
%
where $F_j$ is the fission-neutron source integrated over region $j$ and the
coefficient $a_{ij}$ is the expected number of fission neutrons born in region
$i$ per fission neutron emitted in region $j$. The $N\times N$ array of these
coefficients is the fission matrix $\mathbf{A}$: its dominant eigenvalue is
$k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and the associated eigenvector is the region-wise fission
source. Since each coefficient is defined per emitted source neutron, the
fission multiplicity is already embedded in it: a source neutron that induces a
fission contributes $\bar\nu$ neutrons to the destination region.

Two conventions are fixed here once and for all, because both circulate in the
literature. The column index $j$ labels the \emph{source} region and the row
index $i$ the \emph{destination} region, and the source $\mathbf{F}$ is the
\emph{right} eigenvector of $\mathbf{A}$, that is, the matrix acts on the
source vector as written in Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:eigen}). The distinction is not
academic: with the transposed convention the fission source reconstructed from
the same tallies departs from the detector estimate by several per cent, against
a fraction of a per cent with the convention above. Throughout Part II the
eigenproblem is solved with the dense solver of the SciPy library
\cite{VirtanenSciPy2020}; for matrices of the size used here the solution takes
a fraction of a second.

\subsection{Two ways of generating the matrix}\label{fm:sec:generation}

The matrix of one core configuration can be obtained in two ways. The first
assembles it from a set of fixed-source calculations, one for each
``fundamental cell'' of the system \cite{WaltersNSE2018}. The fundamental units
are identified by exploiting the geometrical and material similarity between
repeated units, so that a few calculations serve for the whole core; reflectors
and structural materials are deliberately left out of the tally to preserve
that similarity, and their influence is restored afterwards through boundary
and discontinuity corrections. This is the route taken by the RAPID code
system, a mature implementation that delivers whole-core solutions within
minutes at an accuracy comparable to the underlying Monte Carlo, supported by
dedicated techniques for homogenization \cite{HePNE2020Homog}, temperature
interpolation \cite{HeANE2020Temp} and the treatment of material and
control-rod discontinuities \cite{HeANE2019,HePNE2020,Mascolino2022}. Its cost
is a mesh fine enough to resolve every local effect, which in turn sets the
size of the matrix and the storage it needs; in exchange, the fixed-source
calculations are cheap and the uncertainty of the tallied coefficients is
small once enough histories are run.

The second way tallies the matrix directly during a single whole-core
criticality calculation. Serpent~2 \cite{LeppanenEPJN2025} accumulates the
coefficients on the fly, at negligible additional cost, by recording over the
active cycles the region of birth of every fission neutron against the region
of birth of its parent. The tally uses the converged fission source of the
criticality calculation, so the within-cell distribution of the source is far
better resolved than in a fixed-source run and much larger cells can be used;
reflectors, structural materials and inter-assembly heterogeneity are embedded
in every coefficient, and one calculation yields one complete matrix instead
of a set of responses to be assembled. The price is statistical: the
uncertainty of the coefficients comes down only with the number of histories of
the criticality run, which becomes demanding when pin-wise cells are wanted.

The choice between the two is specific to the design and to the purpose. All
the work of this Part takes the second route. The aim is a straightforward
workflow feeding an online core-monitoring tool, for which assembly-level
resolution is what is needed and pin-wise accuracy is not; a matrix that
already contains the reflector and the drums, tallied from the same model used
for the reference calculations, is the simplest object that serves that aim.

\section{The reactor model}\label{fm:sec:model}

The reactor is the heat-pipe micro reactor developed at Argonne National
Laboratory within the NEAMS Micro-Reactor Application Drivers area
\cite{Stauff2024}, a modelling exercise designed to collect the challenges that
the micro-reactor industry faces at once: heat pipes for the removal of the
nuclear heat, TRISO fuel for operation at very high temperature, yttrium
hydride pins for moderation and rotating drums in the radial reflector for
control. Its Serpent~2 and multiphysics models are distributed on the Virtual
Test Bed \cite{moose_vtb_mrad_description}, and the acronym VTB is used for the
model in what follows.

The core is rated at \SI{2}{MW}. The fuel is TRISO particles of low-enriched
uranium oxycarbide (\SI{19.95}{at\percent} $^{235}$U) embedded in a hexagonal
graphite matrix at a packing fraction of \SI{40}{\percent}; the heavy-metal
inventory is \SI{448.5}{kg}. The active length is \SI{160}{cm}, bounded at the
top and at the bottom by \SI{20}{cm} of beryllium reflector. Thirty fuel
assemblies sit inside a single ring of beryllium radial reflector that houses
the twelve control drums, and a shutdown rod in the centre of the core provides
redundancy. The heat is removed by heat pipes with a stainless steel envelope
and potassium as working fluid. Figure~\ref{fm:fig:vtb} sketches the geometry.

Serpent~2 tallies the fission matrix on a Cartesian mesh. That is convenient
for a square lattice, where the mesh can be made to coincide with the fissile
elements at pin or assembly level, and unsuited to the hexagonal lattice of the
HPMR. The tally is therefore taken per fissile material instead: each fuel
assembly is one material, and each assembly is split into three axial zones to
resolve the axial dependence, so that the mesh has $30\times3 = 90$ cells and
the matrix $90\times90 = 8100$ coefficients. The discretization is fully
three-dimensional. The indices $i$ and $j$ do not label a radial position but a
cell obtained by combining the radial position of an assembly with one of its
axial zones, and every quantity defined on the matrix in the following,
corrections included, connects two such cells, so radial and axial effects are
treated together and not as separate factors. Tallied with the population used
throughout Part II, the coefficients carry a median relative uncertainty of
about \SI{9}{\percent}; that figure, and the populations behind it, are given
with the campaigns of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search}.

\section{Database and interpolation}\label{fm:sec:database}

A matrix describes one state of the core. The method becomes predictive when a
database of matrices, tallied at a set of reference states, is interpolated to
represent a state that was never simulated. The database is paid for once, in
Monte Carlo calculations, and then used any number of times; how many entries
it must contain for a given accuracy is not known in advance, and is settled
for each axis by a convergence study, reported where the axis is introduced.

Each entry is a whole-core calculation at a uniform state: one temperature for
the entire system, fuel, moderator and structures alike, with no spatial
gradient, and, where the drums enter, one rotation angle common to all drums.
The temperature axis used in Part II spans \SIrange{300}{1000}{K} in steps of
\SI{100}{K}, eight levels; the drum axis is described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search}.

An arbitrary state is represented coefficient by coefficient. For a
temperature field that differs from cell to cell, the interpolation of the
coefficient $a_{ij}$ uses the temperature of its \emph{destination} cell $i$,
that is, the row: the coefficient is assumed to depend on the local temperature
of the region where the fission neutrons are born. With a drum angle $\theta$
as second axis, the interpolation is bilinear between the four database entries
that bracket the target point,
%
\begin{equation}
  \tilde a_{ij}(\tilde T_{f,i},\tilde\theta) \;=\;
  \sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1}
  w_T^{(p)}\!\left(\tilde T_{f,i}\right)\,
  w_\theta^{(q)}\!\left(\tilde\theta\right)\,
  a_{ij}^{(d_T+p,\;d_\theta+q)},
  \label{fm:eq:interp}
\end{equation}
%
where $d_T$ and $d_\theta$ index the database levels immediately below the
target values, $a_{ij}^{(d_T+p,\,d_\theta+q)}$ is the coefficient tallied at
the corresponding grid point, and the weights are the linear ones,
$w_T^{(1)} = (\tilde T_{f,i} - T^{(d_T)})/(T^{(d_T+1)} - T^{(d_T)})$ and
$w_T^{(0)} = 1 - w_T^{(1)}$, and likewise for $\theta$. Along a single axis
the scheme reduces to the linear interpolation between the two bracketing
levels, which is the form used when only the temperature varies. The tilde
marks an interpolated quantity from here on: $\tilde{\mathbf A}$ is the
interpolated matrix, and $\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf F}$ its
eigenpair. More elaborate interpolants are possible and were left for future
work; the linear scheme is the simplest that could be tested, and the accuracy
it reaches is the subject of the next two chapters.

\section{Correction ratios}\label{fm:sec:ratios}

The interpolation sees only what the database axes contain. Three effects lie
outside them and are met in Part II. The temperature of the reflector is not an
axis: the coefficients are defined between fissile cells, so the reflector
enters only through the uniform-temperature entries, and a core hotter than its
reflector has no entry to draw on. Sharp temperature gradients between
neighbouring fuel cells are not an axis either, since each entry is isothermal.
And the composition of the fuel, which changes with burnup, is not an axis,
because adding one would multiply the size of the database.

The three works handle the three effects in the same way. A correction ratio
is a ratio of two matrices tallied at reference conditions that differ in the
effect of interest alone, and it is applied to the interpolated matrix
element by element,
%
\begin{equation}
  \tilde a^{*}_{ij} \;=\; \Big[\, w_{ij}\,\big(r_{ij} - 1\big) + 1 \,\Big]\;
  \tilde a_{ij},
  \label{fm:eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
%
where $r_{ij}$ is the ratio and $w_{ij}$ a weight between zero and one that
measures how much of the reference effect is present at the target state. The
weight reduces the correction to the identity where the effect is absent and
applies it in full where the target state coincides with the reference; the
ratio, being a quotient of two tallies of the same quantity, cancels the
systematic errors common to numerator and denominator. The Burnup Correction
Ratio of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} is the case $w_{ij}=1$, with the ratio
interpolated in time between snapshots; the Reflector Correction Ratio and the
Core Temperature Correction Ratio of Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios} differ
in what the ratio compares and in how the weight is built from the temperature
field. Their definitions, and the tests that justify them, are given there.

\section{Statistical uncertainty of the interpolated results}\label{fm:sec:uq}

A result obtained from the interpolated matrix is compared with a reference
Monte Carlo calculation of the same state. Both carry statistical noise, and a
residual discrepancy that falls within that noise cannot be read as a modelling
bias; quantifying the noise on both sides is therefore part of the method. Two
independent sources combine in quadrature,
%
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{X,\mathrm{tot}}^2 \;=\; \sigma_{X,\mathrm{ref}}^2 + \sigma_{X,\mathrm{DB}}^2,
  \label{fm:eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
%
for any observable $X$, be it $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ or the source of one cell. The
first term is the uncertainty of the reference run, which Serpent reports for
the multiplication factor and, through the fission-source detector, for every
cell. The second is less immediate. The interpolated matrix is not the outcome
of a Monte Carlo run of its own: it is a new object assembled from database
entries that carry finite statistics, and it has no replicas from which an
uncertainty could be read off. Its contribution has to be estimated. (The NSE
paper writes $\sigma_{\mathrm{interp}}$ for this term; $\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$ is
used here throughout.)

\subsection{Resampling the database}

The estimate is built by resampling. Every database coefficient is treated as
a Gaussian variable centred on its tallied value with the standard deviation
Serpent reports for it; a complete realization of the database is drawn, and
the whole pipeline is rerun on it, interpolation, correction ratio and
eigenproblem, yielding one realization of $\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and of the
normalized source. Repeating the draw $M$ times, the standard deviation of the
$M$ results is $\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$. Propagating the database covariance
through a linearized sensitivity would be cheaper, but the correction ratios
are nonlinear in the coefficients, which appear in both numerator and
denominator; resampling the full pipeline avoids any linearization. Since the
fission source is normalized to its sum, the relative uncertainty of a
normalized value coincides with that of the corresponding tally, and the
relative quantities of Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:sigma}) combine directly.

The draw is defined by a covariance. Writing the perturbation of the retained
coefficients as $\boldsymbol\delta\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma)$,
the covariance factors as $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf D\,\boldsymbol\rho\,\mathbf D$,
with $\mathbf D$ the diagonal matrix of the per-coefficient standard deviations
read from the database and $\boldsymbol\rho$ the correlation matrix. The
amplitude $\mathbf D$ is fixed by the tallies; what has to be decided is
$\boldsymbol\rho$, and the decision is not innocuous.

\subsection{Why the coefficients of one column are correlated}

Coefficients tallied within a single criticality calculation are not
independent, because the number of histories per active cycle is fixed
\cite{BrissendenGarlick1986}. Consider two coefficients of the same source
column, $a_{ij}$ and $a_{lj}$ with $i\neq l$. At the start of a cycle the
number $N_j$ of source neutrons born in $j$ is inherited from the fission bank.
Each of them, independently of the others, either causes a fission in cell $i$,
with probability $\pi_i$, or elsewhere; in the analog tracking used here a
history is terminated when it causes fission, so it contributes to one
destination only, with all its $\bar\nu$ secondaries born there. The
coefficient is the per-source-neutron average of these contributions, and the
standard multinomial results \cite{CasellaBerger2002} give
%
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(a_{ij}) = \frac{\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i(1-\pi_i)}{N_j},\qquad
  \operatorname{Cov}(a_{ij},a_{lj}) = -\frac{\bar\nu^2\,\pi_i\pi_l}{N_j}.
  \label{fm:eq:multinomial}
\end{equation}
%
The covariance is negative for every pair of destinations sharing a source
column: a fixed number of histories is split, exhaustively and exclusively,
among the destinations, and more of them going to $i$ means fewer going to
$l$. In the correlation the run-time factor $\bar\nu^2/N_j$ cancels, and what
remains depends only on the destination probabilities, which are themselves
readable from the tallied means, $a_i = \bar\nu\,\pi_i$. Writing
$\kappa = -1/\bar\nu$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathrm{pred}}(i,l) \;=\;
  \kappa\,\frac{\sqrt{a_i\,a_l}}{\sqrt{(1+\kappa a_i)(1+\kappa a_l)}},
  \label{fm:eq:rho}
\end{equation}
%
a closed form that needs only $\bar\nu$ and the mean coefficients of the
database itself, no additional Monte Carlo replica. The multiplicity is treated
as a constant and every history is given unit weight; neither simplification
touches the covariance, which vanishes term by term as long as a history
fissions at most once, and their effect on the variance that normalizes it
was quantified at a few parts in $10^{4}$ for the calculations of Part II.

\subsection{Four estimates and their validation}

Four ways of fixing $\boldsymbol\rho$ were compared on the same twelve test
cases, on both $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and the source. (i) \emph{Uncorrelated
resampling}, $\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf I$: every coefficient perturbed on its
own, which is the assumption of earlier FM uncertainty work
\cite{HeWaltersNSE2022} and the natural baseline. (ii) The \emph{analytical
model}, $\boldsymbol\rho=\boldsymbol\rho_{\mathrm{pred}}$ of
Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:rho}), imposed column by column through a Cholesky factor
shared by the coefficients of the same source column. (iii) \emph{Measured
correlation}, obtained from the cycle-by-cycle tallies of one run by treating
each active cycle as a pseudo-replica, which required modifying Serpent to print
the matrix of every active cycle. (iv) \emph{Brute force}: the whole database
rebuilt $150$ times from genuinely independent runs, one per temperature and
repetition, with no model of the covariance at all; the most expensive estimate
and the reference for the other three.

Against brute force, the uncorrelated baseline gives systematically the largest
$\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$ and the analytical model the smallest, with the measured
correlation in between. On $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ the analytical model reproduces
the brute-force uncertainty to within \SI{4.6}{\percent}, ahead of the measured
correlation (\SI{8.7}{\percent}) and well ahead of the uncorrelated baseline
(\SI{18.0}{\percent}): the intra-column correlations are negative, so
neglecting them inflates the propagated variance of the eigenvalue. On the
root-mean-square of the source the measured (\SI{3.3}{\percent}) and the
analytical (\SI{3.6}{\percent}) models both track brute force more closely
than the uncorrelated one (\SI{7.3}{\percent}), the source being more
sensitive than the eigenvalue to the cross-column correlations that the
analytical model leaves out.

One word needs fixing before the results are read, because the three works use
it in opposite senses. A wider uncertainty band is \emph{conservative} for the
uncertainty estimate, since it overstates the noise, and \emph{permissive} as
an acceptance test, since it makes a residual easier to declare compatible with
noise. The uncorrelated baseline is conservative in the first sense, by a
factor of roughly $1.5$ where it was measured against replicates; the
analytical model, giving the smallest $\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$, yields the
narrowest acceptance window and is therefore the strictest test.

The three works draw on this framework with different settings, each stated
where the results appear. The temperature-correction study of
Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios} uses the analytical model with $M=500$
realizations. The reflector-correction study of the same chapter and the
criticality search of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} use the uncorrelated
baseline, with the correction ratio rebuilt inside every realization; the
former with $M=500$, the latter with a fixed ensemble of $M=50$ realizations
drawn once from a fixed seed, truncated at zero and kept in memory, so that
every result of the cycle analysis is evaluated on the same fifty databases.
Fifty was chosen after checking the convergence of $\sigma_k$: with twenty
realizations it is underestimated by 10--15\% and varies by as much between
independent groups of twenty, with fifty it is within \SI{5}{\percent} of its
value at two hundred.

\section{Error metrics}\label{fm:sec:metrics}

Two observables are compared with the reference throughout Part II, the
multiplication factor and the fission source, and the same four measures are
used for them. For the multiplication factor,
%
\begin{equation}
  \Delta k_{\mathrm{eff}} = \big(\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}} - k^{\mathrm{MC}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\big)\times10^{5}
  \quad\text{(pcm)},
  \qquad
  z = \frac{\Delta k_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}},
  \label{fm:eq:dk}
\end{equation}
%
the second being the residual in units of its own uncertainty. The fission
source is normalized to unit sum over the cells, and its local relative
deviation and the root-mean-square of the deviations over the core are
%
\begin{equation}
  \delta\tilde F_i = \frac{\tilde F_i - F_i^{\mathrm{MC}}}{F_i^{\mathrm{MC}}}\times100
  \quad(\%),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\delta\tilde F_i\big)^2}.
  \label{fm:eq:source}
\end{equation}
%
These are plain metrics, chosen over a covariance-weighted distance such as
Mahalanobis's on purpose: they are less sensitive to the spatial pattern of a
residual, and directly interpretable, which is what a case-by-case comparison
needs. Every uncertainty band drawn in the following is $\pm2\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}$,
so that a point falling inside or outside a band coincides with passing or
failing the $|z|<2$ test used as the ``consistent with noise'' criterion. As a
check of the reference side, the reference calculations of the reflector tests
were each repeated three times with different seeds: the root-mean-square
deviations of the source obtained against the three runs are consistent with
one another, and the seed has no visible effect on the comparison.
