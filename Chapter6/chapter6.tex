\chapter{The Fission Matrix Method for Heat-Pipe Micro Reactors}\label{ch:fm-theory}

\section{Motivation and scope}

Heat-pipe micro reactors (HPMRs) are controlled by drums rotating in the radial
reflector. Their design and their operation require the same calculation to be
repeated many times: at a given power and at a given point of the cycle, the
drum angle that makes the core critical, and the fission-source distribution
that corresponds to it. A full-core Monte Carlo calculation answers the
question for one state at a cost of several tens of CPU hours, as quantified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search}. Such a cost is acceptable for a limited number of
states and not for the thousands demanded by a design space, a fuel cycle or an
online monitoring tool.

The fission matrix (FM) method \cite{CarneyANE2014} addresses this limitation.
A single Monte Carlo calculation is made to tally, in addition to its usual
results, a matrix that couples every fissile region of the core to every other.
That matrix contains the eigenvalue and the fission source of the state at
which it was tallied, and it can be stored. A database of matrices tallied at a
set of reference states then represents any intermediate state by
interpolation, at the cost of an eigenvalue problem of modest size: seconds
instead of hours, with no Monte Carlo calculation in the loop.

The three works of this Part build on that idea for the same reactor and with
the same tools. Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} builds the database over the fuel
temperature and the drum angle, uses it for a criticality search, and extends it
along the cycle with a correction for burnup. Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios}
addresses what the interpolation cannot represent, the temperature of the
reflector and sharp temperature gradients inside the core, through two
multiplicative corrections. The present chapter provides the common ground: the
derivation of the fission matrix from the transport equation, the strategies
available for its generation, the reactor model, and the general framework
within which the database, the statistical uncertainty and the error
metrics are defined. The axes and the density of the database, the
interpolation formulae and the corrections applied to the interpolated
matrix are specific to each work, and are introduced in
Chapters~\ref{ch:crit-search} and \ref{ch:correction-ratios} together with
the results that depend on them.

\section{The fission matrix}\label{fm:sec:definition}

\subsection{Derivation from the transport equation}

The starting point is the $k$-eigenvalue form of the neutron transport
equation. For the purpose of the method it is convenient to write the balance
not for the angular flux but for the fission source, that is, for the density
$S(\mathbf r)$ of fission neutrons born per unit volume at the position
$\mathbf r$. A fission neutron born at $\mathbf r'$ undergoes a sequence of
flights, scattering events and, possibly, an absorption that induces a further
fission; the expected number of next-generation fission neutrons that it
produces per unit volume at $\mathbf r$ is denoted by
$H(\mathbf r \leftarrow \mathbf r')$. This kernel contains the entire transport
process, cross sections, angular and energy distributions of the secondaries and
fission multiplicity included, integrated over one generation. Since the
production of fission neutrons is linear in the source that generates them, the
fission source of one generation is obtained from that of the previous
generation by integration over the volume $V$ of the system, and the stationary
condition reads \cite{CarneyANE2014}
%
\begin{equation}
  S(\mathbf r) \;=\; \frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{eff}}}\int_{V}
  H(\mathbf r \leftarrow \mathbf r')\,S(\mathbf r')\,\mathrm d\mathbf r' .
  \label{fm:eq:integral}
\end{equation}
%
The multiplication factor $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the eigenvalue that makes the
source stationary from one generation to the next, and
Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:integral}) is equivalent to the $k$-eigenvalue transport
equation: the angular flux is recovered from $S$ by transporting the source
neutrons, and it is through that transport that the kernel $H$ is defined.

The fissile part of the domain is now partitioned into $N$ disjoint regions
$V_i$, and the source integrated over each region is introduced,
$F_i = \int_{V_i} S(\mathbf r)\,\mathrm d\mathbf r$. Integrating
Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:integral}) over the destination region $V_i$ and splitting the
integral over $\mathbf r'$ into the contributions of the source regions $V_j$
gives
%
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{eff}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N} a_{ij}\,F_j
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{F} = k_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathbf{F},
  \label{fm:eq:eigen}
\end{equation}
%
with
%
\begin{equation}
  a_{ij} \;=\;
  \frac{\displaystyle\int_{V_i}\!\int_{V_j}
        H(\mathbf r \leftarrow \mathbf r')\,S(\mathbf r')\,
        \mathrm d\mathbf r'\,\mathrm d\mathbf r}
       {\displaystyle\int_{V_j} S(\mathbf r')\,\mathrm d\mathbf r'} .
  \label{fm:eq:coef}
\end{equation}
%
The coefficient $a_{ij}$ is the expected number of fission neutrons born in
region $i$ per fission neutron emitted in region $j$, the emitted neutrons
being distributed within $V_j$ according to the source. The $N\times N$ array
of these coefficients is the fission matrix $\mathbf A$. Its dominant
eigenvalue is $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and the associated eigenvector is the
region-wise fission source. Since each coefficient is defined per emitted
source neutron, the fission multiplicity is embedded in it: a source neutron
that induces a fission contributes $\bar\nu$ neutrons to the destination
region. The matrix is non-negative, so that the dominant eigenvalue is real and
positive and its eigenvector has components of one sign, as a physical source
must. Only the fissile regions need to be partitioned: non-fissile materials,
reflectors and control elements emit no fission neutrons and enter the problem
through the kernel, that is, through the value of the coefficients.

Equations~(\ref{fm:eq:eigen}) and (\ref{fm:eq:coef}) are an exact consequence
of Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:integral}). What turns them into a method is the observation
that $a_{ij}$ depends on the source only through its shape inside the region
$V_j$, and neither on its amplitude nor on the source elsewhere. If the regions
are small enough, or the shape within them stable enough, for that internal
shape to be insensitive to the global configuration, the coefficients become
properties of the material and geometrical state of the system, independent of
the solution, and they can be tallied once and reused. This is the assumption
on which the fission matrix method rests \cite{CarneyANE2014}. The deviation
between the within-region source shape at the time of the tally and the shape
in the state being represented is the intrinsic approximation of the method,
and it decreases as the partition is refined.

In a Monte Carlo calculation the coefficients are estimated directly from their
definition. For every fission neutron born in $V_j$, the number of fission
neutrons that its history produces in $V_i$ is scored; the average over all the
source neutrons of $V_j$ is $a_{ij}$. Tallied during a criticality calculation,
the estimate uses the converged fission source of that calculation, so that the
within-region shape entering Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:coef}) is the physical one for
the state being simulated.

\subsection{Conventions}

The notation of Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:eigen}) adopts two conventions that are
maintained throughout Part II. The column index $j$ denotes the source region
and the row index $i$ the destination region; as a consequence the fission
source $\mathbf F$ is the right eigenvector of $\mathbf A$, and the matrix
multiplies the source vector as written. The literature is not uniform on this
point, since the coefficient is sometimes defined with the indices exchanged,
in which case the transposed matrix is used. The two forms are not
interchangeable. Transposition preserves the eigenvalues, so that
$k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is unaffected; the right eigenvector of the transposed
matrix, however, is the left eigenvector of $\mathbf A$, which in this
formalism plays the role of the adjoint source, the importance of a fission
neutron born in each region, and not of the source itself. The point was
verified on the database of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} against the fission
source tallied by Serpent with an independent detector: the right eigenvector
of the matrix, as written by the code, reproduces the detector source with a
mean deviation of \SI{0.3}{\percent}, whereas the eigenvector of the transposed
matrix departs from it by 3 to \SI{9}{\percent}. Throughout Part II the
eigenproblem is solved with the dense solver of the SciPy library
\cite{VirtanenSciPy2020}; for matrices of the size used here the solution takes
a fraction of a second.

\section{Strategies for the generation of the fission matrix}\label{fm:sec:generation}

The matrix of one core configuration can be obtained in two ways. The first
assembles it from a set of fixed-source calculations, one for each
``fundamental cell'' of the system \cite{WaltersNSE2018}. The fundamental units
are identified by exploiting the geometrical and material similarity between
repeated units, so that a limited number of calculations serves for the whole
core; reflectors and structural materials are deliberately excluded from the
tally to preserve that similarity, and their influence is restored afterwards
through boundary and discontinuity corrections. This is the approach of the
RAPID code system, a mature implementation that delivers whole-core solutions
within minutes at an accuracy comparable to that of the underlying Monte Carlo,
supported by dedicated techniques for homogenization \cite{HePNE2020Homog},
temperature interpolation \cite{HeANE2020Temp} and the treatment of material and
control-rod discontinuities \cite{HeANE2019,HePNE2020,Mascolino2022}. The
approach requires a mesh fine enough to resolve every local effect, which in
turn determines the size of the matrix and the storage it requires; in
exchange, the fixed-source calculations are inexpensive and the uncertainty of
the tallied coefficients is small once a sufficient number of histories is run.

The second way tallies the matrix directly during a single whole-core
criticality calculation. Serpent~2 \cite{LeppanenEPJN2025} accumulates the
coefficients during the calculation, at negligible additional cost, by
recording over the active cycles the region of birth of every fission neutron
against the region of birth of its parent. The tally uses the converged fission
source of the criticality calculation, so that the within-cell distribution of
the source is resolved far better than in a fixed-source run and considerably
larger cells can be used; reflectors, structural materials and inter-assembly
heterogeneity are embedded in every coefficient, and one calculation yields one
complete matrix instead of a set of responses to be assembled.

The cost of the second strategy is statistical, and it grows with the spatial
resolution demanded of the matrix. The uncertainty of the coefficients
decreases only with the number of histories of the criticality calculation. The
number of coefficients grows with the square of the number of cells, and the
fraction of the source neutrons of a cell that induce fission in any given
other cell decreases as the cells are refined, so that the number of histories
required to tally every coefficient to a prescribed relative uncertainty grows
faster than the number of cells. At assembly level, for a core of thirty
assemblies, the requirement is met by a criticality calculation of ordinary
size, as the campaigns of Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search} show. The same core
resolved at pin level would multiply the number of cells by the number of fuel
pins per assembly, and the number of coefficients by its square, with a
corresponding growth of the histories required. For a large power reactor such
as a pressurized water reactor, with some two hundred assemblies and several
tens of thousands of fuel pins, a pin-wise matrix tallied in a single
criticality calculation is beyond reach; that regime is precisely where the
fixed-source strategy prevails, because the repetition of the fundamental units
makes the cost of the tally independent of the size of the core
\cite{WaltersNSE2018}.

The choice between the two strategies is therefore specific to the design and
to the purpose. All the work of this Part adopts the second one. The aim is a
direct workflow feeding an online core-monitoring tool, for which
assembly-level resolution is sufficient and pin-wise accuracy is not required;
a matrix that already contains the reflector and the drums, tallied from the
same model used for the reference calculations, is the simplest object that
serves that aim.

\section{The reactor model}\label{fm:sec:model}

The reactor is the heat-pipe micro reactor developed at Argonne National
Laboratory within the NEAMS Micro-Reactor Application Drivers area
\cite{Stauff2024}, a modelling exercise designed to gather in one design the
challenges that the micro-reactor industry faces: heat pipes for the removal of
the nuclear heat, TRISO fuel for operation at very high temperature, yttrium
hydride pins for moderation and rotating drums in the radial reflector for
control. Its Serpent~2 and multiphysics models are distributed on the Virtual
Test Bed \cite{moose_vtb_mrad_description}, and the acronym VTB is used for the
model in what follows.

The core is rated at \SI{2}{MW}. The fuel is TRISO particles of low-enriched
uranium oxycarbide (\SI{19.95}{at\percent} $^{235}$U) embedded in a hexagonal
graphite matrix at a packing fraction of \SI{40}{\percent}; the heavy-metal
inventory is \SI{448.5}{kg}. The active length is \SI{160}{cm}, bounded at the
top and at the bottom by \SI{20}{cm} of beryllium reflector. Thirty fuel
assemblies are arranged inside a single ring of beryllium radial reflector that
houses the twelve control drums, and a shutdown rod in the centre of the core
provides redundancy. The heat is removed by heat pipes with a stainless steel
envelope and potassium as working fluid. Figure~\tref{fm:fig:vtb} shows the
geometry.

Serpent~2 tallies the fission matrix on a Cartesian mesh. That is convenient
for a square lattice, where the mesh can be made to coincide with the fissile
elements at pin or assembly level, and unsuited to the hexagonal lattice of the
HPMR. The tally is therefore taken per fissile material instead: each fuel
assembly is one material, and each assembly is split into three axial zones to
resolve the axial dependence, so that the mesh has $30\times3 = 90$ cells and
the matrix $90\times90 = 8100$ coefficients. The discretization is fully
three-dimensional. The indices $i$ and $j$ do not label a radial position but a
cell obtained by combining the radial position of an assembly with one of its
axial zones, and every quantity defined on the matrix in the following,
corrections included, connects two such cells, so that radial and axial effects
are treated together and not as separate factors. The Monte Carlo populations
used to tally the matrices, and the statistical uncertainty of the coefficients
that results from them, are given with the campaigns of
Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search}.

\section{Parametrization over the operating state}\label{fm:sec:database}

A matrix describes one state of the core. The method becomes predictive when a
database of matrices, tallied at a set of reference states, is interpolated to
represent a state that was never simulated. The operating state is described by
a small number of parameters, the temperature field, the angle of the drums and
the burnup of the fuel in the works of this Part; the database is a grid over
those parameters, or over a subset of them, computed once and used any number
of times. The number of entries required for a given accuracy is not known in
advance and is settled for each parameter by a convergence study,
Secs.~\ref{cs:sec:selection} and \ref{cs:sec:grid}.

Two features of the interpolation are common to all the works and are stated
here. The first is that the state is represented coefficient by coefficient:
each $a_{ij}$ is interpolated with the local value of the parameters that
concern it, not with a single global value. This is what allows a non-uniform
temperature field to be represented from database entries that are, each of
them, isothermal. The second is the assignment of that local value. For a
temperature field that differs from cell to cell, the interpolation of
$a_{ij}$ uses the temperature of its destination cell $i$, that is, of the
row: the coefficient is assumed to depend on the temperature of the region
where the fission neutrons are born, which is where the cross sections that
determine their production are evaluated. The interpolated matrix is denoted by
$\tilde{\mathbf A}$, and the tilde marks an interpolated quantity from here
on; $\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf F}$ are the eigenpair of
$\tilde{\mathbf A}$, obtained by solving Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:eigen}) on the
interpolated matrix, since nothing is interpolated in the eigenvalue or in the
source directly. The interpolation is bilinear in temperature and drum angle
in Chapter~\ref{ch:crit-search}, Eq.~(\ref{cs:eq:interp}), and linear in
temperature in Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios}, Eq.~(\ref{cr:eq:interp1d}).

\section{Statistical uncertainty of the interpolated results}\label{fm:sec:uq}

A result obtained from the interpolated matrix is compared with a reference
Monte Carlo calculation of the same state. Both carry statistical noise, and a
residual discrepancy that falls within that noise cannot be interpreted as a
modelling bias; quantifying the noise on both sides is therefore part of the
method. Two independent sources combine in quadrature,
%
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{X,\mathrm{tot}}^2 \;=\; \sigma_{X,\mathrm{ref}}^2 + \sigma_{X,\mathrm{DB}}^2,
  \label{fm:eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
%
for any observable $X$, whether $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ or the source of one cell.
The first term is the uncertainty of the reference calculation, which Serpent
reports for the multiplication factor and, through the fission-source detector,
for every cell. The second term requires an estimate. The interpolated matrix
is not the outcome of a Monte Carlo calculation of its own: it is a new object
assembled from database entries that carry finite statistics, and it has no
replicas from which an uncertainty could be read.

The estimate adopted in all the works of this Part is an ensemble method. Every
database coefficient is treated as a random variable centred on its tallied
value, with the standard deviation that Serpent reports for it; a complete
realization of the database is drawn, and the whole chain of the method is
rerun on it, interpolation, correction and eigenvalue problem, yielding one
realization of $\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and of the normalized source. The
standard deviation of the results over $M$ realizations is
$\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$. Propagating the covariance of the database through a
linearized sensitivity would be less expensive, but the corrections of
Chapters~\ref{ch:crit-search} and \ref{ch:correction-ratios} are ratios of
tallied coefficients, nonlinear in them because the coefficients appear in
both numerator and denominator;
rerunning the full chain on each realization avoids any linearization. Since
the fission source is normalized to its sum, the relative uncertainty of a
normalized value coincides with that of the corresponding tally, and the
relative quantities of Eq.~(\ref{fm:eq:sigma}) combine directly.

The realizations were drawn, in all three works, by uncorrelated sampling: each
coefficient is perturbed independently of the others, which is also the
assumption of earlier work on the uncertainty of fission-matrix results
\cite{HeWaltersNSE2022}. The assumption is not exact. The coefficients tallied
within one criticality calculation are correlated, because the number of
histories per cycle is fixed and the source neutrons of one cell are
distributed among the destination cells exhaustively; a larger share reaching
one destination implies a smaller share reaching the others. Neglecting these
correlations overestimates $\sigma_{\mathrm{DB}}$, so that the uncorrelated
estimate is conservative, in the sense that it overstates the noise, and
correspondingly permissive as an acceptance test, since it makes a residual
easier to declare compatible with noise. The structure of the correlations,
their analytical modelling and the validation of the resulting estimate against
independent replicas of the whole database are examined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:correction-ratios}, in connection with the core temperature
correction. The number of realizations and the treatment of the ensemble differ
between the works and are stated where the results appear.

\section{Error metrics}\label{fm:sec:metrics}

Two observables are compared with the reference throughout Part II, the
multiplication factor and the fission source, and the same four measures are
used for them. For the multiplication factor,
%
\begin{equation}
  \Delta k_{\mathrm{eff}} = \big(\tilde k_{\mathrm{eff}} - k^{\mathrm{MC}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\big)\times10^{5}
  \quad\text{(pcm)},
  \qquad
  z = \frac{\Delta k_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}},
  \label{fm:eq:dk}
\end{equation}
%
the second being the residual in units of its own uncertainty. The fission
source is normalized to unit sum over the cells, and its local relative
deviation and the root-mean-square of the deviations over the core are
%
\begin{equation}
  \delta\tilde F_i = \frac{\tilde F_i - F_i^{\mathrm{MC}}}{F_i^{\mathrm{MC}}}\times100
  \quad(\%),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\delta\tilde F_i\big)^2}.
  \label{fm:eq:source}
\end{equation}
%
These metrics were preferred to a covariance-weighted distance, such as the
Mahalanobis distance, because they are less sensitive to the spatial pattern of
a residual and directly interpretable, as a case-by-case comparison requires. Every uncertainty band drawn in the following is
$\pm2\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}$, so that a point falling inside or outside a band
coincides with passing or failing the $|z|<2$ test adopted as the criterion of
consistency with the statistical noise.
