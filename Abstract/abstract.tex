\begin{abstract}
The reactor concepts now under development depart significantly from the light-water designs
on which current computational practice was built. New geometries, new energy spectra
and new materials call for calculation schemes that have yet to be established,
for methods able to support them, and for the study that the design and the
licensing of these reactors require. This thesis contributes to that effort on
two concepts, with two research lines each. For lead-cooled fast reactors
(LFRs) the work concerns the multi-group data on which deterministic full-core
calculations rest: the route by which they are generated and corrected, and the
energy grid over which they are condensed. For heat-pipe micro reactors (HPMRs)
it concerns fission matrix (FM) methods: the search for the critical state of
the core along the fuel cycle, and the corrections that bring the method to the
conditions of an operating core. In both cases the reference against which
every result is judged is a Monte Carlo calculation performed for that specific purpose.

The first Part follows these two lines. The first concerns the generation and
the correction of the multi-group data, and is developed on a simplified model
of the Westinghouse LFR design.
The constants are generated with
Serpent~2 along two routes, a single full-core model and a set of mini-core
models representative of the regions of the system, and used in a
finite-element diffusion solver with a superhomogenization correction of the
regions responsible for the largest discrepancies. The mini-core route proves
to be the better choice, for accuracy and for computing time alike, whenever several
control-rod configurations have to be analysed, and the correction brings the
multiplication factor and the power distribution close to the reference.

The second concerns the energy grid, and is developed on the ALFRED core
design, where the energy grid on which the constants are condensed is treated
as the unknown of an optimization process. A neural metamodel trained on fewer
than one hundred thousand full-core evaluations of the FRENETIC nodal diffusion
code replaces the solver inside a genetic
search, so that one thousand independent searches become affordable against the
three that the solver alone allows, and every structure the searches return is
recomputed with the solver.
The dependence of the result on the random seed is measured, the best
fifteen-group structure improves on the three previously published for this
core, which came from three searches with FRENETIC itself inside the loop, and
the optimized structures share an exclusive choice in the fast region and place
their boundaries according to the neutron importance rather than to the flux.
Their quality rests on a cancellation between the condensation error and the
error of the deterministic model, which binds the result to the core, the
solver and the objective, while the procedure carries over.

The second Part develops FM methods for the online monitoring of an HPMR, on
the model distributed on the Virtual Test Bed platform. Along its first line, a
database of matrices over fuel temperature and control-drum angle, with a
prescribed correlation between power and temperature, supports a criticality
search that converges in about one second on a single computational core and returns states
whose multiplication factor lies within a few tens of pcm of a reference Monte
Carlo calculation; a multiplicative burnup correction ratio extends it along
the fuel cycle without the need to add an axis to the database. Along the
second, two further correction
ratios account for the temperature difference between core and reflector and
for sharp temperature gradients inside the core, and the statistical
uncertainty that the interpolated matrices inherit from the tallies is derived
in closed form and verified against independent replicas of the whole
database.
\end{abstract}
